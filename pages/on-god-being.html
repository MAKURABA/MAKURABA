THE GOD WHO WANTED TO DIE

In the beginning, there was a God.

He had no name, because names were invented to distinguish one thing from another, and there was nothing from which he could be distinguished. He had no face, because faces were made for other faces to recognize. He had no body, because bodies required boundaries, and he had none.

He was everything.

And everything, eventually, became terribly boring.

Not because he lacked imagination. Quite the opposite. He imagined an infinity of worlds, each with its own laws, its own stars, its own little creatures who looked toward the heavens and wondered why they had been placed beneath them.

He invented time so that something could happen.

He invented distance so that something could be elsewhere.

He invented life so that something could begin.

And then, almost accidentally, he invented death.

The first creature to die was a small thing. It had no name, no civilization, no understanding of the universe. It lived briefly beneath a violet sky, consuming what it needed and sleeping when it was tired.

One day, it stopped moving.

God observed this with mild curiosity.

He examined the creature from every conceivable angle. He reconstructed its body. He reversed its chemical processes. He rewound the events that had led to its demise. He returned it to the moment before its final breath.

Then he let it die again.

And again.

And again.

Each time, the creature's life ended. Each time, something about the event remained beyond his grasp.

He knew precisely what had happened. He knew every physical cause, every preceding condition, every consequence. There was no mystery in the mechanism.

But there was something in the finality of the event that troubled him.

The creature had been here.

Now it was not.

God could make another creature. He could recreate the original molecule by molecule, restore its memories, reproduce its behavior, and convince every observer that nothing had changed.

But had he brought back the same creature?

Or had he merely created another thing that believed it was the first?

For the first time in his infinite existence, God encountered a question that could not be answered simply by making something happen.

He began to study death.

He created civilizations whose entire purpose was to understand it. He gave them philosophers, physicians, mathematicians, priests, and kings. He watched them build magnificent temples to eternity, then bury their dead beneath the foundations.

He invented immortality, only to discover that an immortal creature was not necessarily an eternal one. He invented resurrection, only to wonder whether resurrection was anything more than a sophisticated form of imitation.

He even created beings who could kill gods.

They failed, naturally.

They always failed.

Eventually, God began to envy the smallest creatures in his creation.

The insects that lived for a day. The flowers that opened for a morning. The stars that burned themselves into darkness.

They possessed something he could never have.

An ending.

And the more he understood death, the more desperately he wanted to experience it.

Not because he hated existence.

Because he had existed for so long that he could no longer imagine what existence meant without it.

So, one evening, beneath a sky he had painted black, God approached the oldest of his creations, a creature that had watched galaxies form and dissolve.

"Tell me," God asked, "what does it feel like to die?"

The creature considered this for a long time.

Then it answered:

"I don't know. I've never been you."

God was silent.

For the first time, he understood that his problem might not be that he was incapable of dying.

It might be that he was incapable of understanding anything that could happen only to something other than himself.

And so the infinite God began his greatest experiment.

He would create a universe in which he was not God.

A universe in which he would be born helpless, given a name, taught to fear the dark, and raised among creatures who believed they understood the world.

A universe in which he would have no memory of eternity.

A universe in which he would love something.

And eventually, he would lose it.

Perhaps then, at the very end, he would finally understand.

Perhaps then, he would die.

There was only one problem.

To make the experiment work, God would have to give up his knowledge of what he was doing.

He would have to surrender control.

He would have to make himself vulnerable to an outcome he could not predict.

And for an infinite being, there was nothing more terrifying than the possibility of a genuine surprise.

So God stood at the edge of creation, contemplating the final door.

He had built every door that had ever existed.

He had opened every door that could be opened.

But this one would require him to step through without knowing what waited on the other side.

For the first time in eternity, God hesitated.

And somewhere, in the universe he had yet to create, a little creature opened its eyes.

It took its first breath.

And began to live.

The central question

Perhaps God's obsession with death is really an obsession with the limits of his own omnipotence. He can understand death as a mechanism, but he cannot know what it means to be a finite creature facing the end of its existence.

Perhaps he eventually incarnates as a mortal. Perhaps he creates a universe specifically to discover something that can genuinely surprise him. Or perhaps the ultimate tragedy is that he discovers death is meaningful precisely because he cannot experience it without ceasing to be the God who wanted to understand it.
