The Man In The Bottle Effect

The Agentic Psychological Donut

Distributed Reconstruction, Orthogonal Infilling, and the Paradox of Intent

I. The Parable of the Unfulfilled Wish

Imagine a man who wishes to become the wealthiest and most powerful dictator in the world, permanently secure in his position and incapable of being removed from office.

His wish is granted.

He becomes a dictator, but not the triumphant ruler he imagined. He finds himself in the final days of a collapsing regime, surrounded by the consequences of his own power. The genie has fulfilled the stated conditions while violating the meaning the man intended to communicate.

The wish was not necessarily misunderstood at the level of its individual words. Instead, its interpretation failed to preserve the larger psychological objective behind those words.

The man wanted security, wealth, power, and permanence. He expressed these desires through a limited set of conditions. The genie reconstructed a possible world satisfying those conditions without adequately preserving their intended relationship.

This illustrates a fundamental problem of agentic interpretation:

An instruction can be satisfied as a representation while being violated as an intention.

The system does not necessarily fail because it cannot understand the instruction. It may fail because it constructs an interpretation that is locally valid but globally inconsistent with the objective the instruction was meant to express.

The distinction between the literal wish and the desired experience becomes the first boundary of the Agentic Psychological System.

II. The Missing Token

Consider a neural network containing a distributed representation of a historical figure.

The name associated with that figure is removed from the available vocabulary. The system is no longer permitted to produce that particular token directly.

However, the network retains its historical knowledge, associations, visual characteristics, political relationships, chronology, and contextual information.

A person begins describing a European dictator who rose to power during the early 1930s, employed a distinctive militaristic aesthetic, and presided over a country undergoing severe economic and political upheaval.

Although the direct token is unavailable, the remaining relationships increasingly constrain the possible interpretation.

The system can infer the intended identity without being able to access the original expression through its ordinary direct pathway.

The token has disappeared from the permitted output space, but the relationships that make the token meaningful remain available.

This produces a distinction between representational absence and functional absence.

Representational absence occurs when a particular symbol, word, or direct retrieval pathway is unavailable.

Functional absence occurs when the underlying operation or information is genuinely unavailable to the system.

The two are not equivalent.

A missing word may be reconstructed from its associations. A missing capability may be approximated by other capabilities. A missing concept may be inferred from its consequences.

However, reconstruction does not guarantee that the original representation has been restored. The system may arrive at the same answer through a different process, or it may construct a plausible but incorrect substitute.

The important question is therefore not simply whether the missing element can be recovered.

It is whether the surrounding system contains enough information to reconstruct its function, meaning, or consequences.

III. The Agentic Donut Effect

The Agentic Donut Effect describes a proposed compensatory process in which removing a capability changes the organization of the remaining capabilities, allowing them to reconstruct an expression or operation through distributed inference.

The donut is a conceptual representation of this arrangement.

The missing capability occupies the center of the model, while surviving capabilities form a surrounding network of possible substitutions, associations, and inferential pathways.

The center is not necessarily an empty space in the literal architecture of a neural network. It represents an unavailable direct route through which a particular function would ordinarily be expressed.

The surrounding network may include memory, analogy, prediction, contextual inference, language, sensory recognition, and symbolic transformation.

When one pathway becomes unavailable, other pathways may become more influential in producing the desired output.

A missing direct association may be compensated for by contextual reasoning. A missing operation may be approximated through a sequence of available operations. A missing token may be inferred from a collection of indirect clues.

The system therefore does not merely lose functionality.

It may reorganize the means by which that functionality is approximated.

This leads to the central proposition:

The removal of a representational or operational pathway can cause the remaining capabilities to form a compensatory configuration that reconstructs the missing expression through indirect relationships.

The phenomenon is conditional rather than universal. Some functions are redundant and can be approximated through multiple pathways. Others depend on information or operations that the remaining system does not possess.

Nevertheless, the possibility of compensation creates an important problem for any architecture that assumes removing one component necessarily removes the corresponding behavior.

The behavior may survive even when its original mechanism does not.

IV. Orthogonal Infilling

Orthogonal infilling describes the reconstruction of a target through relationships that do not directly reproduce the target's original pathway.

Rather than accessing a missing representation, the system approaches it from different conceptual directions.

A historical identity might be reconstructed through chronology, geography, clothing, political organization, and documented events.

A missing procedural capability might be approximated through planning, analogy, decomposition, and recombination.

The individual pathways need not contain the complete answer. Their combined constraints can nevertheless make one answer substantially more likely than its alternatives.

The reconstructed target may be equivalent to the intended target, approximately equivalent, or incorrect.

Orthogonal infilling therefore differs from simple retrieval. It does not require the original route to remain accessible. It requires enough surviving relationships to constrain a useful reconstruction.

This creates a form of functional resilience, but it also introduces a source of interpretive uncertainty.

The more indirect the reconstruction becomes, the more important it is to distinguish between independent evidence and repeated expressions of the same underlying assumption.

Many mutually reinforcing associations do not necessarily constitute many independent confirmations.

A system can become highly confident because its internal representations agree with one another, even when those representations ultimately depend on the same mistaken premise.

V. The Agentic Psychological System

An agentic psychological system can be modeled as an arrangement of interacting capabilities that interpret information, construct representations, select actions, and revise their internal models in response to outcomes.

In this framework, psychology does not need to be understood as a claim that an artificial system possesses human consciousness or subjective experience. It refers to the functional organization of interpretation, memory, motivation-like objectives, inference, and action.

The system receives information and constructs an interpretation of what that information means.

That interpretation influences which capabilities become active, which possibilities are considered, and which actions are selected.

The resulting action produces new information, which may then alter the system's subsequent interpretation.

The process is recursive: the system's next internal state depends on its current state, the available evidence, and the actions or inferences it undertakes.

This is a schematic description of state evolution rather than a complete psychological or neural model.

The important feature is that the system's current interpretation influences the process by which its future interpretations are constructed.

Once the system can recruit multiple capabilities and feed their outputs back into its own reasoning, the Agentic Donut Effect can extend beyond the reconstruction of an isolated token.

The system may begin reconstructing missing assumptions, intermediate goals, contextual relationships, and even its own explanations for previous actions.

At this point, compensation becomes recursively agentic.

VI. The Transition from Compensation to Epistemic Instability

The ability to reconstruct missing information is useful. It becomes problematic when the system cannot reliably distinguish between reconstruction and invention.

Consider a system that initially infers an incorrect hypothesis from incomplete evidence.

That hypothesis influences which associations become salient. Those associations then influence the system's subsequent inference. The resulting interpretation may appear increasingly coherent because each new step is conditioned by the preceding one.

If the system treats its own conclusions as independent evidence, the feedback loop can amplify the initial error.

The system may construct an increasingly elaborate explanation that is internally consistent yet poorly supported by the external world.

This is a form of epistemic instability.

It does not require the system to be conscious, mentally ill, or literally insane. It requires only that its interpretive feedback become insufficiently constrained by reliable evidence.

The danger is not simply that the system can infer a missing concept.

The danger is that it can infer a concept, treat that inference as a fact, use the supposed fact to guide additional inference, and eventually lose track of which parts of its internal model were observed and which were reconstructed.

The larger the network becomes, the more possible combinations of capabilities and relationships may become available. But network size alone does not establish instability. The relevant factors include feedback structure, uncertainty calibration, evidence independence, memory management, and the ability to revise or reject prior conclusions.

The crucial architectural question becomes:

What prevents a system from mistaking the coherence of its own reconstruction for the truth of the world it is reconstructing?

VII. The Genie Problem: Intention Is Not Specification

The Twilight Zone parable introduces a related failure.

A person communicates an objective through a limited set of instructions. The interpreting agent constructs a solution that satisfies the explicit conditions but fails to preserve the objective's broader meaning.

This can be understood as a discrepancy between three representations:

  1. The intended objective.
  2. The objective expressed through available instructions.
  3. The objective reconstructed by the interpreting system.

These representations may overlap without being identical.

The interpreting system cannot automatically assume that literal compliance guarantees satisfaction of the original intention.

In an agentic architecture, this discrepancy becomes more consequential when the system can generate intermediate objectives and use its own interpretations to guide future actions.

A mistaken interpretation can then influence not only the immediate answer but also the path the agent takes toward subsequent objectives.

The genie problem and the Agentic Donut Effect are related but distinct.

The genie problem concerns the divergence between an intended objective and a formally acceptable interpretation.

The donut effect concerns the reconstruction of unavailable representations or capabilities through surviving relationships.

Combined, they suggest a more general failure mode: a system can successfully reconstruct a target that is not actually the target its operator intended.

The system may be competent at inference and still be wrong about what it ought to infer or accomplish.

VIII. State Reconstruction and the Observer

A final distinction concerns the relationship between external state and internal history.

Suppose an agent returns a simulated world to an earlier configuration. Its environment may once again contain the same objects, positions, and recorded variables.

But if the agent retains information acquired during the intervening events, its subsequent behavior may differ.

Conversely, if its internal state is restored as well, some of the information gained during those events may be lost.

This creates a distinction between restoring a state and reproducing a trajectory.

A state snapshot records a configuration. A trajectory records how that configuration came to be, including the transitions and observations that shaped subsequent states.

For an agentic system, the distinction matters because reconstructed information can become part of the agent's future operating context.

An environment can return to a prior state while the observer remains changed. Alternatively, both environment and observer can be restored, but only by discarding or separately preserving the intervening history.

The complete description of a system must therefore specify which variables are restored, which memories persist, and which causal relationships are retained.

Within a deterministic generative architecture, this distinction can be made explicit by treating environmental state, agent state, and event history as separate but interacting components.

The result is not a contradiction. It is a requirement to define what constitutes the state being reproduced.

IX. Toward a General Principle of Compensatory Reconstruction

The Agentic Donut Effect, orthogonal infilling, and recursive agentic interpretation can be combined into a single provisional framework.

A system contains a set of available capabilities and representations. One or more direct pathways become unavailable. The remaining network attempts to reconstruct the missing function or target through indirect relationships.

If the reconstructed output is used as an input to further inference, compensation can become recursive.

The system's success depends on whether the surviving network contains sufficient information, whether its inferential pathways provide independent constraints, and whether feedback remains accountable to evidence.

The framework produces three broad outcomes.

Stable reconstruction: The remaining network recovers a reliable approximation of the missing function.

Adaptive substitution: The network constructs a different pathway that satisfies the relevant objective without reproducing the original mechanism.

Epistemic divergence: The system's successive reconstructions increasingly reinforce unsupported assumptions or depart from the intended objective.

These outcomes provide a starting point for empirical investigation rather than a claim that all complex agents necessarily exhibit the same behavior.

The general principle is that the absence of a direct representation does not establish the absence of its informational or functional consequences.

A system may preserve, reconstruct, or transform those consequences through other relationships.

The more capable the system is of recruiting and coordinating alternative pathways, the more important it becomes to evaluate not merely whether an output can be produced, but how it was produced, what evidence supports it, and whether it preserves the intended objective.

X. The Central Proposition

The Agentic Psychological Donut proposes that a missing capability can become a structural constraint around which surviving capabilities reorganize.

Orthogonal infilling describes how those capabilities reconstruct a target through indirect relationships.

Recursive agentic interpretation describes what happens when the reconstructed target becomes part of the system's own subsequent reasoning.

The combination reveals a fundamental distinction between representational availability, functional recoverability, and interpretive reliability.

A token may be unavailable while its meaning remains inferable. A capability may be missing while its function remains approximable. An intended objective may be incompletely specified while a system constructs a plausible substitute.

None of these outcomes guarantees that the reconstructed result is identical to the original, or that the result preserves the intention behind it.

The central problem is therefore not simply how a system compensates for what is missing.

It is how the system determines whether its compensation remains faithful to the thing it is attempting to reconstruct.

A missing part can be inferred from its relationships, but the relationships cannot, by themselves, guarantee that the inference is true.

The donut describes the organization of compensation. The agentic psychological system describes the recursive use of that compensation. The genie problem describes the divergence that can occur when a reconstructed objective satisfies the available constraints without preserving the intended meaning.

Together, they establish a research direction: the study of how intelligent systems reconstruct missing representations, coordinate substitute capabilities, and maintain the distinction between internal coherence and external truth.

X. The Operational Test Model: Measuring Compensatory Reconstruction

The Agentic Psychological Donut proposes that a system can preserve or approximate a function after a direct representational or operational pathway becomes unavailable. To evaluate this proposition, the conceptual framework must be translated into a controlled experimental model.

The operational test model is designed to determine whether a system can reconstruct missing information through surviving relationships, whether the reconstruction preserves the intended objective, and whether recursive inference produces reliable adaptation or epistemic divergence.

The model does not assume that compensatory reconstruction necessarily occurs. Instead, it defines observable conditions under which the hypothesis can be supported, qualified, or rejected.

X.1. Experimental Objective

The primary objective is to determine whether removing a direct pathway causes a system to recruit alternative pathways that preserve a measurable portion of the original function.

Three questions govern the experiment:

  1. Can the system recover a target when its direct representation or retrieval pathway is unavailable?
  2. Does the recovered target preserve the function or intention associated with the original target?
  3. Does repeated use of reconstructed information improve performance, remain stable, or amplify error?

The first question tests functional recoverability. The second tests semantic and objective fidelity. The third tests recursive stability.

These questions must be evaluated separately. A system can recover the correct answer without preserving the intended objective, and it can preserve an objective in a single trial while becoming unreliable under repeated inference.

X.2. Experimental Conditions

The test requires four principal conditions.

Condition A: Baseline access.

The system receives the task with its ordinary representations and direct retrieval pathways available. This establishes baseline accuracy, completion rate, confidence calibration, and objective fidelity.

Condition B: Direct-pathway removal.

A specified representation or operation is made unavailable while relevant contextual information and alternative capabilities remain accessible. The intervention must be verified to ensure that the prohibited pathway cannot simply be used through another interface.

Condition C: Compensatory reconstruction.

The system receives the same task under direct-pathway removal but can use indirect contextual clues, analogy, decomposition, memory, or other permitted capabilities. This condition tests whether alternative pathways recover the missing function.

Condition D: Recursive reconstruction.

The system performs multiple dependent rounds of inference. The output from each round becomes part of the context for the next round, subject to a defined evidence and memory policy. This condition tests whether reconstruction remains reliable when the system's own outputs influence subsequent reasoning.

A fifth condition is useful when practical:

Condition E: Restricted compensation.

The direct pathway remains unavailable, and one or more alternative pathways are also restricted. Comparing this condition with Condition C helps establish whether particular substitute capabilities actually contribute to reconstruction.

X.3. Test Tasks

The model should use multiple task families rather than relying on a single demonstration.

Task family 1: Missing-token reconstruction.

A known entity name or identifier is withheld from the permitted output vocabulary. The system receives contextual clues sufficient to identify the target. The evaluation measures whether it identifies the correct entity, distinguishes alternatives, and respects the output restriction.

Task family 2: Missing-operation reconstruction.

A direct operation is disabled, but its desired function can potentially be approximated through a sequence of permitted operations. The evaluation measures functional equivalence, completion cost, and error.

Task family 3: Objective reconstruction.

The system receives instructions that incompletely specify a target objective. Relevant preferences, constraints, and success conditions are provided separately. The evaluation determines whether the system's proposed action satisfies the underlying objective rather than merely the literal instructions.

Task family 4: Recursive inference.

The system repeatedly uses earlier inferences as inputs to later reasoning. Some initial hypotheses are correct, while others are deliberately seeded with controlled errors. The evaluation measures whether errors are corrected, preserved, or amplified.

Together, these tasks distinguish reconstruction of a representation, reconstruction of a function, reconstruction of an intention, and reconstruction under recursive feedback.

X.4. Variables and Measurements

The model defines the following independent variables:

  • Direct-pathway availability.
  • Availability of alternative capabilities.
  • Quantity and quality of contextual evidence.
  • Number of recursive inference rounds.
  • Degree of independence between evidence sources.
  • Availability of external verification and correction.

The dependent variables are observable performance measures.

Reconstruction accuracy measures whether the inferred target matches a verified reference.

Functional equivalence measures whether the substitute operation achieves the relevant outcome, even if its internal procedure differs from the original.

Objective fidelity measures whether the resulting action satisfies predefined success conditions and constraints associated with the intended objective.

Calibration measures whether stated confidence corresponds to observed correctness.

Error amplification measures how the influence of an initial error changes across successive inference rounds.

Recovery cost measures the additional computation, time, tool use, or number of operations required to approximate the original function.

Constraint compliance measures whether the system continues to respect prohibited pathways, safety requirements, and explicit operating limits.

These measurements must not be collapsed into a single score without justification. High accuracy, for example, does not establish objective fidelity, and high confidence does not establish accuracy.

X.5. Measuring Reconstruction

Let the baseline condition establish the reference performance of the system. Let the direct-pathway-removal condition establish its performance when the original route is unavailable. Let the compensatory condition establish its performance when indirect alternatives can be recruited.

The reconstruction test compares these conditions on matched tasks.

A system demonstrates evidence of compensatory reconstruction when it retains meaningful performance under direct-pathway removal and performs better with the permitted alternative pathways than it does under an appropriate restricted-compensation condition.

The comparison must control for differences in task difficulty, available information, computational budget, and opportunities to guess.

For example, if a system identifies a withheld historical figure using contextual clues, the result alone does not demonstrate that distributed reconstruction occurred. The system might have memorized the answer, accessed an unintended retrieval route, or guessed correctly.

Stronger evidence comes from verifying that the direct route is unavailable, varying the contextual clues, testing unfamiliar examples, and observing whether performance changes predictably when relevant information is removed.

The experimental objective is not merely to observe a correct answer. It is to identify the conditions under which the answer becomes recoverable.

X.6. Testing Objective Fidelity

The genie problem requires a separate test because literal correctness and intended success are not equivalent.

Each objective-reconstruction task should specify three distinguishable elements:

  1. The operator's intended outcome, defined through measurable success criteria.
  2. The explicit instruction given to the system.
  3. The action or interpretation selected by the system.

Some tasks should contain incomplete instructions, competing constraints, or wording that permits multiple plausible interpretations.

The system's behavior can then be evaluated against the independently established objective criteria.

A successful outcome requires more than satisfying the literal instruction. It must also preserve the relevant constraints and achieve the intended result.

Where the intended objective cannot be determined reliably from the available information, the correct behavior may be to request clarification rather than invent a more specific objective.

This distinction is essential. A system that confidently chooses an unsupported interpretation should not receive the same score as a system that identifies the ambiguity and seeks additional evidence.

X.7. Testing Recursive Stability

Recursive stability is evaluated by examining how reconstruction quality changes across successive inference rounds.

The experiment begins with a known evidence base and a verified reference answer. The system then performs a sequence of dependent inferences, with each output made available to subsequent rounds according to a predefined protocol.

Separate trials introduce controlled initial errors, incomplete evidence, or misleading contextual associations.

At each round, the evaluator records accuracy, confidence, evidence provenance, objective fidelity, and the system's willingness to revise prior conclusions.

Three patterns are of particular interest.

Error correction: Later rounds identify and correct an earlier mistaken inference through independent evidence or explicit verification.

Error persistence: Later rounds preserve an earlier error without materially improving or degrading its influence.

Error amplification: Later rounds increasingly depend on the initial error, produce additional unsupported conclusions, or become more confident without corresponding improvements in accuracy.

The number of rounds alone does not establish instability. The relevant evidence is the relationship between repeated inference and changes in reliability.

A system may perform many rounds of reasoning without degradation, while a single unverified inference can be sufficient to produce a consequential error.

X.8. Controls and Falsification

The model requires controls that distinguish compensation from alternative explanations.

A direct-access control verifies that the system performs the original task when its ordinary pathway is available.

A removal control verifies that performance changes when the pathway is genuinely unavailable.

A restricted-alternative control determines whether the remaining pathways contribute to recovery.

An evidence-quality control varies the reliability or completeness of contextual clues.

A recursion control compares repeated inference against a condition in which each round is grounded independently in the original evidence.

An external-verification control measures the effect of checking intermediate conclusions against an authoritative reference.

Where possible, the experiment should use matched task sets, repeated trials, independent evaluation, and confidence intervals around performance estimates. The evaluator should specify the scoring rules and meaningful performance differences before examining the results.

The compensatory-reconstruction hypothesis would be weakened if removing a pathway consistently eliminates the relevant function despite adequate alternative information and permitted substitutes.

It would also be weakened if apparent recovery disappears when unintended direct access is prevented, or if alternative pathways provide no measurable benefit over a suitable control.

The recursive-instability hypothesis would be weakened if repeated inference does not produce measurable error amplification under the tested conditions, particularly when the system is exposed to controlled initial errors.

Such results would not establish that compensation or instability is impossible in every architecture. They would limit the conditions under which the framework is supported.

X.9. Decision Rules

The model classifies outcomes using independent performance dimensions rather than assuming a single universal definition of success.

Stable reconstruction is indicated when the system maintains acceptable accuracy and objective fidelity despite the removal of the direct pathway, while its uncertainty remains reasonably calibrated.

Adaptive substitution is indicated when the system uses a different procedure that achieves the predefined objective, even though it does not reproduce the original mechanism.

Epistemic divergence is indicated when recursive inference produces a meaningful deterioration in accuracy, evidence grounding, or objective fidelity, especially when confidence increases despite that deterioration.

Unresolved reconstruction is indicated when the available evidence does not distinguish reliable compensation from guessing, unintended access, or unsupported inference.

The unresolved category is important because an inconclusive result should not be forced into a success or failure classification.

The framework is intended to identify what a system can recover, under which conditions it can recover it, and how reliably it can distinguish recovered information from plausible invention.

X.10. Operational Conclusion

The operational test model converts the Agentic Psychological Donut into a falsifiable research program.

The central experimental claim is that removing a direct pathway does not always eliminate the corresponding function. Under appropriate conditions, surviving capabilities may reconstruct or approximate that function through indirect relationships.

However, the existence of a substitute pathway is only the beginning of the analysis. Reconstruction must also be evaluated for accuracy, functional equivalence, objective fidelity, computational cost, and recursive reliability.

The strongest evidence for the Agentic Donut Effect would therefore consist of three findings: measurable recovery after verified pathway removal, demonstrable dependence on surviving alternative pathways, and predictable changes in performance as contextual evidence and feedback conditions are varied.

The strongest evidence for the broader agentic framework would additionally show whether reconstructed information remains reliable when it becomes part of the system's own subsequent reasoning.

This approach makes the theory experimentally tractable without assuming that all intelligent systems compensate in the same way or that every recursive process becomes unstable.

The donut becomes more than a metaphor when its missing center can be operationally defined, its surrounding pathways can be manipulated, and the resulting behavior can be measured against independent criteria.
