HUMAN FALLACY

THE HUMAN FALLACY CONJECTURE

A Conjecture Concerning the Structural Origins, Propagation, Persistence, and Correction of Human Error

MAKURABA | 2026

Classification: Foundational Philosophical Conjecture Research Domains: Human Psychology, Epistemology, Cognitive Science, Logic, Sociology, Systems Theory, Artificial Intelligence.

ABSTRACT

The Human Fallacy Conjecture proposes that human fallibility is an emergent property of cognitive organisms attempting to interpret, predict, and manipulate an environment through incomplete information, finite processing capacity, inherited assumptions, emotional incentives, social conditioning, and imperfect mechanisms of observation.

It proposes that human error cannot be adequately understood by examining individual mistakes in isolation. Instead, fallacy must be investigated as a multilevel phenomenon encompassing the individual mind, interpersonal relationships, collective belief systems, institutions, cultures, and the historical transmission of knowledge.

The conjecture distinguishes between the existence of an error, the mechanism producing the error, the recognition of the error, and the capacity to correct it.

It further proposes that intelligence does not necessarily reduce fallibility. Under certain conditions, increased intelligence may increase an individual's ability to rationalize, defend, reproduce, and institutionalize an incorrect conclusion.

Consequently, the ability to reason must be distinguished from the ability to reason correctly, and the ability to recognize truth must be distinguished from the ability to recognize one's own failure to recognize truth.

The conjecture introduces the possibility that the most persistent human fallacies are not those that cannot be disproven, but those whose underlying mechanisms prevent their holders from accepting disconfirmation.

It therefore advances a central research objective:

To determine whether human correctness can be made increasingly inevitable through the systematic examination, externalization, triangulation, and correction of cognitive error.

I. FOUNDATIONAL DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this conjecture, the following terminology is established.

  1. Human Fallibility: The inherent possibility that a human cognitive process, judgment, perception, interpretation, or decision may produce an inaccurate or unjustified result.
  1. Fallacy: An error in reasoning, inference, interpretation, or argumentation. A fallacy may arise from invalid logical structure, insufficient evidence, hidden assumptions, or improperly applied reasoning.
  1. Cognitive Error: A discrepancy between an individual's mental representation of a phenomenon and the phenomenon as it exists independently of that representation.
  1. Epistemic Limitation: A limitation affecting the acquisition, interpretation, validation, or communication of knowledge.
  1. Cognitive Bias: A systematic tendency in cognition that influences judgment or information processing in a particular direction.
  1. Rationalization: The construction or selection of explanations that make an existing belief, decision, or behavior appear justified, potentially without accurately representing the mechanisms that produced it.
  1. Confirmation Mechanism: Any cognitive, social, or institutional process that preferentially accepts, preserves, or amplifies information compatible with an existing belief.
  1. Disconfirmation: The identification of evidence, observations, or arguments that challenge the validity of an existing proposition.
  1. Epistemic Recovery: The process by which an individual or collective system identifies an error, examines its origin, revises its assumptions, and establishes a more reliable conclusion.
  1. Recursive Fallibility: The possibility that the mechanisms used to identify and correct an error may themselves contain errors.
  1. Observational Independence: The degree to which an observation or evaluation is independent of the assumptions, incentives, expectations, and conclusions of the original observer.
  1. Epistemic Humility: The recognition that one's knowledge, interpretations, and conclusions remain subject to uncertainty and revision.

II. THE FOUNDATIONAL CONJECTURE

We propose:

Human cognition is a fallible interpretive mechanism operating within an environment of incomplete information. Its conclusions are therefore provisional representations rather than guaranteed reproductions of objective reality.

From this proposition, several consequences follow:

  1. Human perception cannot be assumed to provide complete access to reality.
  2. Human memory cannot be assumed to preserve events without alteration.
  3. Human reasoning cannot be assumed to operate independently of emotional or biological incentives.
  4. Human intelligence cannot be assumed to prevent irrational conclusions.
  5. Human confidence cannot be treated as evidence of correctness.
  6. Human consensus cannot independently establish objective truth.
  7. Human institutions cannot be presumed immune to the limitations of their participants.
  8. Human methods of correcting error cannot be presumed free from error themselves.

The conjecture does not establish that human knowledge is impossible.

Rather, it proposes that reliable knowledge requires mechanisms capable of identifying the limitations of the processes through which knowledge is obtained.

The distinction is fundamental.

An imperfect instrument can produce reliable measurements if its limitations are understood, calibrated, and independently examined.

The human mind should therefore be investigated not as an inherently reliable authority, but as an instrument requiring continuous epistemic calibration.

III. THE SEVEN PRIMARY SOURCES OF HUMAN FALLACY

We propose seven interacting domains from which human fallibility emerges.

  1. Perceptual Fallacy

Human beings do not experience the entirety of their environment.

They receive incomplete sensory information, interpret it through existing neurological structures, and construct internal representations of external phenomena.

Perception is therefore not equivalent to reality.

Possible manifestations include:

  • Optical illusions.
  • Auditory misinterpretation.
  • Selective attention.
  • Sensory limitations.
  • Perceptual completion.
  • Misidentification.
  • Context-dependent perception.
  • False recognition.
  • Inaccurate spatial or temporal estimation.

Proposition 1.1: An individual's perception of a phenomenon cannot, by itself, establish the complete nature of that phenomenon.

Proposition 1.2: The subjective certainty of a perceptual experience does not necessarily correspond to its objective accuracy.

Proposition 1.3: Multiple independent observational methods may increase the reliability of conclusions derived from incomplete sensory information.

The implication is that observation must be distinguished from interpretation.

Seeing something is not identical to understanding what has been seen.

  1. Cognitive Fallacy

Human cognition operates under finite computational, attentional, and memory constraints.

To manage complexity, the brain employs shortcuts, approximations, learned patterns, and predictive mechanisms.

These mechanisms can be useful while simultaneously producing systematic errors.

Examples include:

  • Availability heuristic.
  • Anchoring effect.
  • Confirmation bias.
  • Representativeness heuristic.
  • Hindsight bias.
  • Overconfidence effect.
  • Framing effect.
  • Fundamental attribution error.
  • Survivorship bias.
  • Illusory correlation.
  • Neglect of base rates.
  • Planning fallacy.
  • Sunk-cost fallacy.
  • Dunning-Kruger effect, with the qualification that popular interpretations often oversimplify the underlying research.

Proposition 2.1: Cognitive efficiency and epistemic accuracy are not necessarily equivalent.

Proposition 2.2: A cognitive mechanism may be adaptive under one set of environmental conditions and systematically misleading under another.

Proposition 2.3: The repetition of a cognitive process does not independently establish its reliability.

A shortcut that works frequently may become an unquestioned assumption.

The danger emerges when usefulness is mistaken for universal correctness.

  1. Emotional Fallacy

Human reasoning is influenced by emotional conditions.

Fear, anger, affection, shame, pride, grief, pleasure, anxiety, and the desire for belonging may influence the selection and interpretation of information.

This does not mean emotion is inherently irrational.

Emotions can communicate important information concerning danger, attachment, social relationships, and personal values.

However, emotional significance and factual accuracy are separate properties.

Proposition 3.1: The emotional intensity associated with a belief does not independently establish the truth of that belief.

Proposition 3.2: An individual may reject accurate information when accepting it threatens an emotionally important identity, relationship, or psychological structure.

Proposition 3.3: Emotional regulation may improve the conditions under which reasoning occurs, but emotional neutrality cannot be presumed to guarantee correctness.

The objective is not to eliminate emotion.

It is to prevent emotional pressure from becoming an unexamined substitute for evidence.

  1. Linguistic Fallacy

Human beings use language to organize, transmit, and manipulate conceptual representations.

Language is indispensable to collective intelligence, but it can also distort the phenomena it describes.

Words may compress complex phenomena into simplified categories.

Definitions may change.

Metaphors may become mistaken for literal explanations.

Labels may replace investigation.

Possible manifestations include:

  • Equivocation.
  • Ambiguous terminology.
  • False dichotomies.
  • Loaded language.
  • Semantic drift.
  • Category errors.
  • Reification.
  • Circular definitions.
  • Misleading framing.
  • Confusion between a description and the thing described.

Proposition 4.1: A linguistic representation cannot be assumed to contain every property of the phenomenon it represents.

Proposition 4.2: Disagreement may arise from differences in definitions rather than differences in underlying observations.

Proposition 4.3: The precision of terminology does not necessarily correspond to the precision of understanding.

A sophisticated vocabulary can conceal an inadequate model.

Likewise, a simple description can sometimes communicate an accurate observation.

  1. Social Fallacy

Human cognition develops within social environments.

Individuals inherit assumptions, traditions, expectations, authorities, and shared narratives from other individuals.

Consequently, beliefs may be maintained because of their social utility rather than their evidentiary strength.

Examples include:

  • Groupthink.
  • Conformity.
  • Authority bias.
  • Bandwagon effect.
  • Pluralistic ignorance.
  • In-group favoritism.
  • Out-group homogeneity bias.
  • Social proof.
  • Collective rationalization.
  • Reputation-driven reasoning.
  • Diffusion of responsibility.

Proposition 5.1: The number of individuals accepting a proposition does not independently establish its truth.

Proposition 5.2: Social agreement may increase confidence without proportionally increasing evidentiary support.

Proposition 5.3: A group may collectively reinforce an error that would have been more readily identified by an individual operating independently.

Proposition 5.4: Dissent may serve an epistemically valuable function when it introduces genuinely independent examination.

This does not mean consensus is worthless.

Scientific consensus, for example, may reflect extensive independent investigation, reproducibility, and accumulated evidence.

The distinction is between consensus produced through examination and consensus produced through social pressure.

  1. Institutional Fallacy

Institutions are created to coordinate human activity, preserve knowledge, distribute authority, and establish procedures.

However, institutions are themselves composed of fallible individuals and processes.

Their structures may introduce incentives that preserve errors.

Possible mechanisms include:

  • Bureaucratic inertia.
  • Conflicts of interest.
  • Authority preservation.
  • Measurement distortion.
  • Incentive misalignment.
  • Organizational groupthink.
  • Institutional self-protection.
  • Suppression of inconvenient evidence.
  • Procedural formalism.
  • Historical precedent treated as unquestionable authority.

Proposition 6.1: Institutional longevity does not independently establish institutional correctness.

Proposition 6.2: An institution may become increasingly efficient at preserving a conclusion without becoming increasingly accurate.

Proposition 6.3: Procedures intended to prevent error may themselves become sources of error when they are insulated from examination.

Proposition 6.4: Institutional reliability requires mechanisms through which institutional conclusions can be independently challenged and corrected.

An institution should therefore be judged not merely by the number of errors it avoids, but by its capacity to discover and recover from the errors it inevitably produces.

  1. Metacognitive Fallacy

The final domain concerns the individual's ability to evaluate their own cognitive processes.

Human beings may recognize that other people are biased while failing to identify the same mechanisms within themselves.

This introduces a recursive problem.

The mind attempting to evaluate its own reliability is the same mind whose reliability is being evaluated.

Proposition 7.1: The ability to identify fallacies in others does not independently demonstrate an equivalent ability to identify fallacies in oneself.

Proposition 7.2: Confidence in one's own objectivity may itself become an object of investigation.

Proposition 7.3: A system of epistemic correction must examine not only its conclusions but also the procedures through which those conclusions are evaluated.

Proposition 7.4: No self-evaluation procedure should be presumed immune to recursive fallibility.

The observer is not exempt from observation.

IV. THE PARADOX OF INTELLIGENCE

We propose a distinction between intelligence and epistemic reliability.

Intelligence may increase the ability to construct explanations, identify patterns, manipulate abstract concepts, defend existing conclusions, develop persuasive arguments, discover supporting evidence, and construct increasingly sophisticated models.

However, these abilities do not necessarily increase the willingness or capacity to reject an incorrect conclusion.

Indeed, increased reasoning capacity may sometimes increase the sophistication of rationalization.

This produces the following conjecture:

The development of intelligence does not necessarily produce a corresponding development in epistemic humility, and may increase the potential complexity of self-deception when critical examination is absent.

The highly intelligent individual may possess more elaborate mechanisms for defending an incorrect belief.

The educated individual may possess more sophisticated language with which to rationalize an inadequate conclusion.

The experienced individual may mistake familiarity for comprehensive understanding.

The authoritative individual may mistake the ability to command obedience for the ability to establish truth.

Therefore:

Intellectual sophistication must not be treated as a substitute for independent verification.

This proposition does not imply that intelligence is detrimental.

Rather, it suggests that intelligence requires complementary mechanisms of correction.

V. THE SELF-PRESERVATION HYPOTHESIS

We propose that certain human fallacies persist because the acceptance of contrary evidence carries perceived costs.

These costs may be psychological, emotional, social, economic, political, reputational, institutional, or existential.

A belief may become connected to an individual's identity, relationships, livelihood, or understanding of the world.

Consequently, abandoning the belief may appear more threatening than maintaining it.

We therefore propose:

The persistence of a belief may be influenced not only by the strength of its supporting evidence but also by the perceived consequences of relinquishing it.

This introduces two questions:

  1. What evidence supports this belief?
  2. What would it cost me to discover that this belief is incorrect?

The second question does not establish whether the belief is false.

It identifies a potential source of resistance to correction.

A person may defend an incorrect belief because they have invested years in it.

A community may preserve a harmful practice because abandoning it threatens its historical identity.

An institution may reject corrective evidence because acknowledging the error threatens its authority.

In each case, the preservation mechanism may become stronger than the original justification.

VI. THE PROPAGATION OF FALLACY

We propose that human error can propagate through successive layers of social organization.

A possible propagation sequence is:

  1. Individual Misinterpretation: An observation produces an inaccurate conclusion.
  2. Personal Conviction: The conclusion becomes an established belief.
  3. Social Transmission: The belief is communicated and adopted by others.
  4. Collective Reinforcement: Agreement increases perceived legitimacy.
  5. Institutional Incorporation: The belief enters procedures, policies, and traditions.
  6. Historical Normalization: The belief becomes difficult to distinguish from established reality.

This is a proposed explanatory model, not a universal sequence.

Fallacies may also disappear before reaching later stages, and institutions can correct inherited errors.

Nevertheless, the model identifies a significant possibility:

An error may acquire authority through transmission without acquiring accuracy through verification.

Propagation does not constitute validation.

VII. THE FALLACY OF CERTAINTY

We propose that certainty should be treated as a psychological or epistemic state rather than an independent indicator of truth.

An individual may be:

  • Correct and uncertain.
  • Correct and confident.
  • Incorrect and uncertain.
  • Incorrect and confident.

All four conditions are possible.

Consequently, confidence and correctness must be measured independently.

Proposition 7.1: Increased confidence without corresponding increases in evidentiary support may indicate epistemic instability.

Proposition 7.2: An individual's inability to imagine an alternative explanation does not establish that no alternative explanation exists.

Proposition 7.3: The absence of recognized uncertainty does not establish the absence of uncertainty.

Proposition 7.4: A proposition should remain open to revision in proportion to the limitations of its supporting evidence.

This does not require universal indecision.

Decisions must sometimes be made under uncertainty.

The objective is calibrated confidence, not permanent hesitation.

VIII. THE THIRD OBSERVER PRINCIPLE

We propose that one of the most effective ways to investigate human fallibility is to introduce independent examination.

A person evaluating their own reasoning may overlook assumptions that become apparent to another observer.

Two individuals may share the same assumptions and therefore reproduce the same error.

A third observer may introduce a different perspective, identify an overlooked variable, or expose a structural weakness in the original reasoning.

We propose the following three-part arrangement:

Observer One: Original interpretation.

Observer Two: Independent counterexamination.

Observer Three: Examination of both interpretations, including their shared assumptions and methodological limitations.

The third observer must not automatically be considered correct.

Otherwise, the mechanism simply transfers unquestioned authority from one observer to another.

The third observer must also remain subject to examination.

Proposition 8.1: Independent observation may increase the probability of identifying errors when the observers possess sufficiently independent assumptions, information sources, and methods.

Proposition 8.2: The addition of observers does not guarantee correctness when their errors are correlated.

Proposition 8.3: The reliability of collective judgment depends partly on the independence and diversity of its evaluative mechanisms.

The purpose is not to establish an infallible observer.

It is to create a structure in which no observer possesses unquestionable authority.

IX. THE RECURSIVE CORRECTION PROBLEM

If human beings are fallible, then their methods of correcting fallibility must also be considered fallible.

This produces a recursive problem:

  • A person identifies an error.
  • The person develops a correction.
  • The correction relies upon assumptions.
  • Those assumptions may contain errors.
  • A further examination may be required.

The process can continue indefinitely.

We propose that epistemic improvement does not require the elimination of every possible uncertainty.

It requires the establishment of procedures through which errors become increasingly detectable, traceable, and correctable.

A system should therefore prioritize:

  1. Transparency of assumptions.
  2. Traceability of conclusions.
  3. Reproducibility of observations.
  4. Independent verification.
  5. Explicit uncertainty.
  6. Revision mechanisms.
  7. Preservation of contradictory evidence.
  8. Accountability for consequential decisions.

The objective is not an impossible state of absolute cognitive perfection.

It is a progressively more reliable process of inquiry.

X. THE HUMAN FALLACY EQUATION

We propose the following conceptual model.

Let:

  • F = Probability of an erroneous conclusion.
  • P = Perceptual limitations.
  • C = Cognitive limitations.
  • E = Emotional influence.
  • L = Linguistic distortion.
  • S = Social influence.
  • I = Institutional reinforcement.
  • M = Metacognitive limitations.
  • V = Independent verification.
  • R = Effective error correction.

A preliminary conceptual relationship is:

F ∼ f(P, C, E, L, S, I, M)

And:

F(residual) = g(F, V, R)

Where increased independent verification and effective correction are hypothesized to reduce residual error under appropriate conditions.

These expressions are not established empirical equations. They identify variables for future operationalization and testing.

Importantly, the variables are not necessarily independent.

Emotional influence may affect social conformity. Social conformity may influence institutional reinforcement. Institutional reinforcement may influence the information available to individual observers.

The conjecture therefore anticipates a network of interacting variables rather than a simple additive model.

A future mathematical treatment could investigate these relationships through Bayesian inference, causal modeling, information theory, network theory, cognitive science, and computational simulations.

XI. THE FALLACY OF MORAL CERTAINTY

Human fallibility also has consequences for morality.

People frequently make judgments about the intentions, knowledge, culpability, and moral status of others.

However, such judgments are often made using incomplete information.

We propose:

The certainty with which an individual condemns another does not necessarily correspond to the accuracy of the individual's understanding of the circumstances being judged.

This introduces several distinctions:

  • Harm and intention are not identical.
  • Consequence and motivation are not identical.
  • Ignorance and deliberate deception are not identical.
  • Error and malice are not identical.
  • Responsibility and omniscience are not identical.
  • Explanation and justification are not identical.

A person may cause harm without intending harm.

A person may intend harm while misunderstanding its consequences.

An individual may possess accurate information but make an unethical decision.

Another may make an incorrect decision despite acting in good faith.

These distinctions do not eliminate responsibility.

They improve the precision with which responsibility is investigated.

Proposition 11.1: Moral evaluation should distinguish between observable consequences, available knowledge, intention, capacity, and the mechanisms through which a decision was produced.

Proposition 11.2: The recognition of human fallibility must not become an excuse for avoiding accountability.

Proposition 11.3: Accountability mechanisms should themselves remain open to independent scrutiny.

Understanding why an error occurred does not automatically excuse the error.

Likewise, condemning an outcome does not automatically establish that its underlying mechanism has been understood.

XII. THE FALLACY OF AUTHORITY

Authority is a mechanism through which humans coordinate action, establish rules, and distribute responsibility.

However, authority can create an epistemic hazard when obedience becomes confused with verification.

We propose:

An authority's capacity to enforce a conclusion does not independently establish the correctness of that conclusion.

This applies to political, religious, scientific, educational, corporate, technological, legal, and algorithmic authority.

Expertise should be respected where supported by demonstrated competence and evidence.

However, expertise must remain distinguishable from infallibility.

A scientist may be mistaken.

A judge may be mistaken.

A teacher may be mistaken.

A machine may be mistaken.

A majority may be mistaken.

A dissenting individual may also be mistaken.

The solution is not to eliminate authority, but to make authority answerable to evidence, transparent procedures, and mechanisms of correction.

XIII. THE FALLACY OF PROGRESS

Human societies often assume that technological, economic, or intellectual advancement corresponds to moral and epistemic advancement.

This assumption requires examination.

Technological capability can increase without an equivalent increase in wisdom.

Information availability can increase without an equivalent increase in understanding.

Communication speed can increase without an equivalent increase in deliberative quality.

Institutional complexity can increase without an equivalent increase in institutional reliability.

We therefore propose:

The expansion of human capability does not necessarily imply the improvement of human judgment.

A society may become more powerful while retaining ancient errors.

A civilization may develop increasingly sophisticated instruments while continuing to misinterpret the information those instruments provide.

Progress must therefore be evaluated across multiple dimensions rather than represented by a single measurement.

XIV. THE MACHINE AND THE HUMAN FALLACY

The conjecture may be extended to artificial intelligence and other computational systems.

An artificial system can inherit, reproduce, amplify, or introduce errors through:

  • Incomplete training data.
  • Incorrect assumptions.
  • Defective objectives.
  • Biased datasets.
  • Faulty inference.
  • Poorly specified instructions.
  • Inadequate evaluation.
  • Distribution shifts.
  • Misleading feedback.
  • Optimization against inappropriate proxies.

Artificial systems may also produce persuasive explanations for incorrect outputs.

This creates an important research distinction between the apparent confidence of a system and the demonstrated reliability of its mechanisms.

We propose:

The ability of an artificial system to produce coherent explanations does not independently establish the accuracy of its underlying processes.

Likewise, the fact that an error originates from a machine does not establish that its mechanism is fundamentally different from errors originating in human cognition.

The comparison must be empirical, not anthropomorphic.

Human and artificial systems may share certain structural problems while differing substantially in their mechanisms.

This suggests a broader research domain:

Comparative Fallibility: The investigation of how different cognitive and computational architectures generate, preserve, detect, and correct errors.

No system should be exempted from examination merely because of its biological or artificial origin.

XV. THE PRINCIPLE OF NON-EXEMPTION

We propose the following principle:

No observer, institution, intelligence, methodology, or authority should be exempt from the examination of its own mechanisms of error.

This principle applies universally within the scope of the conjecture.

It rejects the assumption that any participant in an epistemic system possesses automatic immunity from fallibility.

It does not imply that all claims deserve equal credibility.

Evidence, competence, reproducibility, and demonstrated reliability remain relevant.

The principle instead establishes that credibility is earned through examination rather than granted through identity or authority alone.

XVI. TESTABLE PREDICTIONS

Although this work begins as a philosophical conjecture, it should generate empirical questions.

The following predictions are proposed for investigation.

Prediction 1: Confidence Calibration

Individuals may exhibit measurable discrepancies between subjective confidence and objective accuracy across different domains.

Prediction 2: Identity Attachment

Beliefs associated with personally important identities may demonstrate different correction patterns from beliefs carrying fewer identity-related consequences.

Prediction 3: Independent Examination

Independent evaluators may identify errors that are overlooked by the original decision-maker, particularly when their information sources and assumptions differ.

Prediction 4: Correlated Observers

Adding more evaluators may produce limited improvements when those evaluators share the same information sources, incentives, and cognitive assumptions.

Prediction 5: Rationalization Capacity

Greater argumentative or reasoning ability may improve the defense of an existing position without necessarily improving the ability to evaluate that position impartially.

This prediction requires careful controls for education, expertise, motivation, and domain knowledge.

Prediction 6: Institutional Persistence

Organizations with weak correction mechanisms may preserve demonstrably inaccurate procedures longer than organizations with transparent review and revision processes.

Prediction 7: Linguistic Precision

Explicit definitions and operational criteria may reduce certain categories of disagreement arising from ambiguity.

Prediction 8: Emotional Conditions

Emotional arousal may influence judgment accuracy differently depending on the task, individual, and nature of the emotion.

Prediction 9: Metacognitive Training

Training that encourages confidence calibration, explicit uncertainty, and examination of counterevidence may improve performance on some reasoning tasks.

Prediction 10: Recursive Verification

Repeated examination may reduce certain categories of error, but improvements may diminish when successive evaluations depend upon the same underlying assumptions.

These predictions are not asserted as proven results. Their purpose is to establish a research program capable of supporting, modifying, or falsifying the conjecture.

XVII. EXPERIMENTAL RESEARCH PROGRAM

A rigorous investigation could proceed through the following stages.

Experiment A: Individual Fallibility

Present participants with reasoning tasks involving incomplete information, ambiguous observations, misleading patterns, and conflicting evidence.

Measure initial conclusions, confidence levels, accuracy, response time, willingness to revise, and ability to identify the source of error.

Objective: Establish measurable relationships between confidence, accuracy, and correction behavior.

Experiment B: Social Reinforcement

Create groups receiving identical evidence but different social conditions.

Some groups receive unanimous initial opinions. Others receive conflicting opinions or independent evaluations.

Measure agreement rates, accuracy, revision frequency, dissent tolerance, and confidence changes.

Objective: Determine how social consensus affects judgment independently of evidentiary quality.

Experiment C: Third Observer Intervention

Compare three conditions:

  1. Individual self-evaluation.
  2. Two-person disagreement.
  3. Three-party evaluation with an independent third observer.

Measure the frequency of identified errors and the accuracy of final conclusions.

Control for the independence of information sources and evaluators.

Objective: Investigate whether triangulated examination produces measurable improvements.

Experiment D: Identity and Belief Preservation

Present participants with propositions carrying different degrees of personal or social significance.

Introduce credible disconfirming evidence.

Measure evidence acceptance, belief revision, confidence changes, argumentative resistance, and recognition of uncertainty.

Objective: Investigate the relationship between identity attachment and epistemic correction.

Experiment E: Institutional Correction

Simulate organizations making repeated decisions under uncertainty.

Compare organizations with no formal review, internal review, independent review, rotating independent review, transparent error reporting, and explicit revision procedures.

Objective: Determine which institutional structures improve error detection and correction.

Experiment F: Human-Machine Comparative Fallibility

Present equivalent reasoning tasks to human participants and artificial systems.

Compare accuracy, confidence calibration, error types, sensitivity to misleading information, response to disconfirmation, reproducibility, and correction behavior.

Objective: Investigate similarities and differences between biological and artificial mechanisms of fallibility.

XVIII. LIMITATIONS AND CONDITIONS OF FALSIFICATION

The Human Fallacy Conjecture must not become an all-encompassing explanation that interprets every disagreement as proof of human irrationality.

Such a theory would reproduce the very problem it seeks to investigate.

The conjecture must therefore establish boundaries.

First: Not every disagreement is a fallacy. Different conclusions may arise from genuinely different evidence, values, assumptions, or objectives.

Second: Not every cognitive shortcut is defective. Heuristics can produce highly effective judgments under appropriate conditions.

Third: Not every emotionally influenced decision is inaccurate. Emotions can provide relevant information.

Fourth: Not every consensus is socially manufactured. Independent convergence can be strong evidence.

Fifth: Not every authority is unreliable. Expertise can substantially improve judgment.

Sixth: Not every error is preventable. Some environments contain irreducible uncertainty.

Seventh: The conjecture itself must remain falsifiable.

Its specific predictions must be tested independently.

If the proposed relationships fail to appear under appropriately controlled conditions, the corresponding propositions must be revised or rejected.

If competing explanations better account for the observed phenomena, those explanations must be considered.

The conjecture must never use its own premise of human fallibility as an excuse to dismiss criticism.

That would constitute a self-protecting epistemic failure.

XIX. THE HUMAN FALLACY CORRECTION PROTOCOL

We propose a practical procedure for identifying and correcting human error.

The Eight-Stage Epistemic Recovery Cycle

Stage 1: Observation

Identify what was actually observed, separating direct information from interpretation.

Stage 2: Determination

Formulate the conclusion and explicitly identify its assumptions.

Stage 3: Counterexamination

Search for evidence that challenges the conclusion.

Stage 4: Triangulation

Introduce independent observers, methods, and sources.

Stage 5: Mechanism Examination

Investigate how the original conclusion was produced, including cognitive, emotional, social, and institutional influences.

Stage 6: Correction

Modify or reject the conclusion where the evidence warrants it.

Stage 7: Verification

Test whether the corrected model produces more reliable results.

Stage 8: Recursive Review

Examine the correction process itself for newly introduced errors.

The procedure is repeatable. Completion does not imply permanent immunity from error.

The cycle establishes a distinction between being wrong and remaining wrong.

The first may be unavoidable.

The second may be reduced through appropriate mechanisms.

XX. THE CENTRAL PHILOSOPHICAL IMPLICATION

If human beings are fallible, then the pursuit of truth requires more than the accumulation of conclusions.

It requires the development of mechanisms capable of evaluating the reliability of the processes producing those conclusions.

A civilization that accumulates information without developing methods of correction may accumulate errors alongside its knowledge.

A civilization that develops increasingly powerful technologies without improving its capacity for epistemic examination may increase the consequences of its own misunderstandings.

A civilization that treats authority as a substitute for verification may institutionalize its mistakes.

A civilization that punishes the admission of error may create incentives for the concealment of error.

Conversely, a civilization that permits correction, protects independent examination, and distinguishes uncertainty from incompetence may improve its capacity for collective understanding.

The objective is not to create a humanity incapable of being wrong.

It is to create conditions in which being wrong does not have to become a permanent condition.

XXI. THE FINAL CONJECTURE

We consolidate the preceding propositions into the following statement:

THE HUMAN FALLACY CONJECTURE

Human fallibility is an emergent property of finite cognitive systems operating within environments of incomplete information, interacting with biological, psychological, linguistic, social, and institutional mechanisms.

Because human beings cannot independently guarantee the accuracy of their own observations, interpretations, and conclusions, no individual or collective reasoning process should be presumed inherently immune to error.

The persistence of human fallacy is hypothesized to depend not only upon the mechanisms that initially produce incorrect conclusions, but also upon the mechanisms through which those conclusions are defended, transmitted, reinforced, normalized, and protected from disconfirmation.

Intelligence, education, confidence, authority, consensus, and technological sophistication may improve particular aspects of human reasoning without independently guaranteeing epistemic reliability.

Consequently, the development of reliable human knowledge requires complementary systems of observation, independent verification, counterargument, metacognitive examination, transparent uncertainty, and recursive correction.

The conjecture further proposes that the introduction of genuinely independent observers may improve the detection of errors, provided that their assumptions, information sources, and evaluative mechanisms are not excessively correlated.

The correction of fallibility must itself remain subject to examination, because the mechanisms employed to identify and correct an error may introduce additional errors.

Therefore, the objective of epistemic development should not be the establishment of an unquestionable authority or an imagined state of perfect human cognition.

It should be the progressive construction of systems in which incorrect conclusions become increasingly detectable, their underlying mechanisms increasingly understandable, their consequences increasingly accountable, and their correction increasingly achievable.

The measure of an intelligent system should not be merely its capacity to produce correct conclusions, but also its capacity to discover when its conclusions are incorrect, understand why they became incorrect, and correct them without requiring the destruction of the observer, the suppression of evidence, or the preservation of an unjustified authority.

XXII. FOUNDATIONAL AXIOMS

The conjecture concludes with twelve provisional axioms for further investigation.

  1. Observation is not equivalent to understanding.
  2. Confidence is not equivalent to correctness.
  3. Intelligence is not equivalent to wisdom.
  4. Consensus is not equivalent to truth.
