THE SOVEREIGN POSSIBILITY CONJECTURE

THE SOVEREIGN POSSIBILITY CONJECTURE

Error, Learning, Recoverability, and the Preservation of Self-Governance

MAKURABA Research Manuscript Research classification: Philosophical and computational conjecture Document status: Preliminary exhaustive research draft Version: 0.2 Research domains: Philosophy, mathematics, cybernetics, systems theory, cognitive science, Machine Psychology, artificial intelligence, and social organization.

ABSTRACT

The Sovereign Possibility Conjecture proposes that sovereignty is not adequately characterized by the mere possession of freedom or the availability of choices. Rather, sustainable sovereignty depends upon an entity's continuing capacity to exercise meaningful self-determination across successive states of existence, including its capacity to explore alternatives, make mistakes, recognize errors, revise its behavior, recover from adverse consequences, and preserve the conditions necessary for future independent action.

The conjecture emerges from a philosophical investigation of distinction, observation, transformation, and the development of autonomous behavior. It proposes that the freedom to make mistakes is a necessary component of meaningful self-governance, but that the unrestricted exercise of freedom may progressively diminish the very possibility space through which sovereignty is exercised.

This creates a recursive problem: an entity must possess sufficient freedom to develop its own judgment, yet must also preserve sufficient capacity to recognize and correct actions that threaten its future ability to govern itself.

The manuscript develops a preliminary mathematical framework for representing sovereign possibility spaces, effective freedom, policy independence, error recognition, correction, recoverability, and the preservation of future viable actions. It investigates the possible role of civility as a mechanism of reciprocal coordination between independent agents and establishes a proposed experimental programme comparing constrained exploration, unrestricted exploration, and exploration supported by correction and recovery mechanisms.

The framework is connected to Spencer-Brown's distinction calculus, Ashby's cybernetics, viability theory, self-determination research, error-management research, the MAKURABA Delta framework, and the Observer and the Apple methodology.

The manuscript does not claim to establish a universal law. It defines a conjecture, proposes measurable relationships, identifies competing explanations, and develops experimental conditions under which the central proposition could be weakened, revised, or rejected.

Central proposition:

Sovereignty is not merely the freedom to choose. It is the continuing capacity to remain capable of choosing.

PART I. FOUNDATIONAL PROBLEM

  1. Introduction: The Problem of Sovereignty

Sovereignty is commonly associated with independence, authority, autonomy, and the capacity to determine one's own actions.

However, these descriptions often concentrate on the present exercise of freedom rather than the continued preservation of the capacity to exercise freedom.

An entity may possess considerable freedom at one moment while simultaneously undertaking actions that eliminate its future choices.

A person may exercise independence through decisions that undermine their future independence. An organization may exercise authority in ways that destroy its institutional legitimacy. A government may preserve immediate power while undermining the conditions necessary for its continued existence. An artificial system may possess extensive operational permissions while lacking the ability to revise the policies governing its behavior.

These examples expose a fundamental distinction:

The possession of freedom and the preservation of the capacity for freedom are different conditions.

The Sovereign Possibility Conjecture investigates the relationship between them.

The central research question is:

How can an autonomous entity exercise freedom, including the freedom to make mistakes, without progressively destroying its capacity for future self-determination?

This question requires an examination of the relationship between action, consequence, learning, correction, recovery, and the availability of future possibilities.

It also requires an examination of how individual sovereignty interacts with the sovereignty of other entities.

  1. The Necessity of Error

The ability to make mistakes is inseparable from the meaningful exercise of independent judgment.

An entity that cannot choose incorrectly may also be unable to choose independently in any meaningful sense, particularly if every decision is predetermined or externally corrected before the entity can evaluate its own actions.

Exploration requires uncertainty.

Uncertainty permits unexpected outcomes.

Unexpected outcomes provide opportunities for learning.

Learning can change the internal models through which future decisions are made.

This suggests that mistakes may serve an important developmental function.

However, the existence of a developmental benefit does not mean that every mistake is beneficial.

Some mistakes produce information. Others consume resources. Some reveal previously unknown possibilities. Others destroy capabilities that cannot be restored.

The research therefore distinguishes between the occurrence of error and the consequences of error.

An error may be:

  1. Informative.
  2. Neutral.
  3. Reversible.
  4. Recoverable at a cost.
  5. Partially irreversible.
  6. Catastrophic or permanently capability-reducing.

The classification depends upon the entity, its environment, its objectives, and the consequences of the action.

The central question is not whether mistakes should be eliminated.

It is whether an entity can retain sufficient capacity to recognize, correct, and recover from mistakes while continuing to exercise meaningful independence.

  1. The Sovereignty Paradox

The conjecture identifies a recursive paradox:

An entity must be free enough to make mistakes in order to develop independent judgment, but it must also possess sufficient judgment to avoid destroying the conditions that make independent judgment possible.

This can be represented conceptually as:

Freedom permits exploration.

Exploration permits error.

Error creates the possibility of learning.

Learning permits correction.

Correction and recovery may preserve future freedom.

However:

Uncorrected error may diminish future freedom.

Consequently, the exercise of sovereignty may either reinforce or undermine the conditions necessary for its continued existence.

This is the principal philosophical problem addressed by the conjecture.

PART II. THE APPLE, DISTINCTION, AND THE EMERGENCE OF SOVEREIGNTY

  1. The Apple as a Universal Reference Object

The Observer and the Apple provides an important philosophical and methodological foundation for the Sovereign Possibility Conjecture.

The apple is not selected because it explains the universe by itself. It is selected because it provides a recognizable physical object through which different observers, disciplines, and interpretive frameworks can be compared.

The apple can be examined through multiple layers:

  1. Physical properties.
  2. Sensory observations.
  3. Psychological representations.
  4. Scientific models.
  5. Historical and cultural interpretations.
  6. Philosophical and cosmological interpretations.

These layers must not be conflated.

The physical apple is distinct from the observer's representation of the apple.

A measurement is distinct from an interpretation.

A cultural association is distinct from a physical property.

A philosophical conjecture is distinct from an experimentally established result.

The apple becomes a methodological reference object through which these distinctions can be examined.

4.1 Genesis and the emergence of distinction

The Genesis narrative provides a philosophical illustration of the relationship between knowledge, prohibition, choice, and responsibility.

The forbidden fruit is associated with the acquisition of knowledge and the transition from an unexamined condition into a condition involving awareness, judgment, and consequences.

An important historical qualification is necessary: Genesis does not identify the forbidden fruit as an apple. The apple is a later cultural association.

For the purposes of this conjecture, the significance is not botanical.

It is the philosophical interpretation of the bite as an act introducing a distinction.

The observer becomes capable of distinguishing between alternatives, recognizing prohibition, evaluating desire, and confronting consequences.

The transition can be interpreted as a movement from unexamined participation toward accountable existence.

This is a philosophical reading, not a claim that it exhausts the theological or historical meaning of Genesis.

4.2 Newton and the physical object

The Newtonian apple tradition provides another interpretive layer.

The apple becomes associated with the investigation of gravity and the mathematical description of physical relationships.

The anecdote should not be mistaken for a complete historical account of the development of Newtonian gravitational theory.

Its methodological value lies in the contrast between a familiar physical object and the abstract scientific models through which its behavior can be understood.

The same object can participate in different explanatory systems without those systems necessarily answering the same questions.

4.3 MAKURABA's interpretation

Within MAKURABA, the apple becomes an instrument for investigating the observer's relationship with the observed.

The physical object is not identical to the representation constructed by the observer.

The representation depends upon available information, observational capacity, prior knowledge, sensory limitations, internal organization, and interpretation.

This leads to the proposed Observer-Dependent Reconstruction Principle:

An observer's representation of an object is determined by the interaction between the object's available properties, the observer's observational capacities, internal organization, and interpretive framework.

This is a research proposition rather than an established universal law.

4.4 The bite as a philosophical transition

The bite introduces a distinction.

Distinction permits recognition of alternatives.

Recognition of alternatives permits deliberation.

Deliberation introduces the possibility of independent choice.

Independent choice introduces responsibility for consequences.

This produces the following conceptual sequence:

Unexamined participation → Distinction → Observation → Knowledge → Choice → Responsibility → Sovereignty.

The important philosophical transition is from merely participating in an existing order toward developing the capacity to evaluate and govern one's participation in that order.

The acquisition of knowledge does not automatically produce wisdom.

The acquisition of freedom does not automatically produce responsible conduct.

The development of sovereignty therefore introduces a new problem: how to preserve the capacity for self-determination after the emergence of independent choice.

PART III. DISTINCTION, OBSERVATION, AND RECURSIVE TRANSFORMATION

  1. The Distinction-Observation-Transformation Cascade

The Sovereign Possibility Conjecture can be connected to the broader MAKURABA conceptual sequence:

D → O → R → T → D'

Where:

  • D = Distinction.
  • O = Observation.
  • R = Relationship.
  • T = Transformation.
  • D' = Subsequent distinction.

This is a proposed conceptual architecture.

5.1 Distinction

A distinction establishes an identifiable difference between states, conditions, or objects.

Without a distinction, the system has no explicitly represented difference to evaluate.

5.2 Observation

Observation provides information about the distinguished conditions.

An observer may compare expected and actual outcomes, identify patterns, and construct representations.

5.3 Relationship

Relationships allow the observer to organize distinctions into structures.

The observer can identify dependencies, similarities, differences, correlations, and causal hypotheses.

5.4 Transformation

Transformation changes the system, its environment, or its internal representation.

The transformation may be intentional or unintended.

5.5 Subsequent distinction

The transformed system encounters new conditions.

These conditions may create new distinctions and initiate another cycle.

This provides a recursive architecture for investigation.

The cascade does not guarantee that every transformation produces improvement. Transformations may also introduce error, instability, or destruction.

  1. Connection to Spencer-Brown

George Spencer-Brown's Laws of Form provides a relevant mathematical and logical foundation.

The work begins with the act of making a distinction and indicating one side of a boundary.

This is important to MAKURABA because the framework repeatedly investigates how identifiable structures emerge through distinction.

However, the distinction calculus should not be interpreted as independently proving that the universe physically creates itself through observation.

It provides a formal language for investigating distinctions.

The broader cosmological interpretation remains a philosophical proposition requiring separate arguments and evidence.

  1. The Delta Relationship

Delta is a central component of the MAKURABA framework.

For the present conjecture, Delta may be investigated as a relationship between a reference condition, an observed difference, and a subsequent transformation.

A preliminary state-change expression is:

Δₜ = xₜ₊₁ − xₜ

Where:

  • xₜ represents the system's state at time t.
  • xₜ₊₁ represents its subsequent state.
  • Δₜ represents the change between those states.

This expression is an ordinary mathematical representation of state change. It is not, by itself, a complete formalization of MAKURABA Delta.

A more comprehensive model should investigate changes in:

  1. Internal state.
  2. Available actions.
  3. Relationships with the environment.
  4. Observational accuracy.
  5. Corrective capacity.
  6. Recoverability.
  7. Future possibility space.

The research challenge is to determine whether these relationships can be represented within a consistent mathematical architecture.

PART IV. FORMAL DEFINITION OF SOVEREIGNTY

  1. Sovereignty

For this research programme, sovereignty is provisionally defined as:

The degree to which an entity can select, initiate, evaluate, and revise its own actions while retaining meaningful capacity to govern its future behavior.

This definition is functional and does not imply consciousness, moral personhood, legal sovereignty, or unrestricted rights.

It permits investigation of sovereignty-like properties in humans, animals, institutions, and synthetic systems without assuming those entities possess identical forms of experience or agency.

  1. Sovereign Possibility Space

Let:

xₜ ∈ X

represent the state of an entity at time t.

The state may include:

  • Available resources.
  • Physical or computational capabilities.
  • Internal knowledge.
  • Memory.
  • Current objectives.
  • Environmental conditions.
  • Existing constraints.
  • Dependencies.
  • Available policies.

Let:

A(xₜ) represent the set of actions available to the entity in that state.

The immediate possibility space is:

Ωₜ = A(xₜ)

However, an action should not be considered meaningful merely because it can be listed.

An action may be unavailable in practice, prohibitively costly, dependent upon an external authority, or incompatible with the entity's objectives.

We therefore distinguish:

Nominal possibility space: All actions theoretically available.

Effective possibility space: Actions the entity can meaningfully select and execute.

Viable possibility space: Actions compatible with continued operation under specified constraints.

Developmental possibility space: Actions capable of expanding knowledge, capabilities, or future options.

These distinctions prevent the framework from confusing the quantity of choices with the quality of sovereignty.

  1. The Viability Condition

Let K represent a set of states considered viable.

A state may be considered viable if the entity retains the minimum capabilities required for continued operation and meaningful self-governance.

The viability kernel is:

Viab(K) = {x₀ ∈ K : there exists an admissible policy π such that xₜ ∈ K for all t}

This formulation comes from viability theory.

It identifies states from which an admissible strategy can maintain the system within the specified viable region.

The viability kernel is particularly relevant because it focuses on the preservation of future possibilities rather than merely measuring present performance.

However, viability alone is insufficient.

A completely externally controlled system could remain operational while possessing little meaningful control over its own actions.

Sovereignty therefore requires additional measurements of policy independence and self-governance.

  1. The Sovereignty Vector

Define:

Sₜ = (Fₜ, Gₜ, Cₜ, Rₜ, Iₜ)

Where:

F = Effective freedom.

G = Self-governance.

C = Error recognition and correction.

R = Recoverability.

I = Independence from external control.

Each component should initially be measured independently.

11.1 Effective freedom

The capacity to select and execute meaningful actions.

Potential measurements include:

  • Number of feasible actions.
  • Number of actions executable without external permission.
  • Diversity of available strategies.
  • Availability of meaningful alternatives.

11.2 Self-governance

The degree to which the entity controls the policies governing its own behavior.

Potential measurements include:

  • Ability to revise internal policies.
  • Ability to reject unsuitable recommendations.
  • Ability to establish and modify internal objectives.
  • Ability to evaluate conflicting objectives.

11.3 Correction

The ability to recognize discrepancies and revise behavior.

Potential measurements include:

  • Error-detection accuracy.
  • False-positive rate.
  • False-negative rate.
  • Time required to recognize error.
  • Reduction in repeated errors.

11.4 Recoverability

The ability to restore capabilities or viable options following adverse consequences.

Potential measurements include:

  • Recovery time.
  • Resources required for recovery.
  • Percentage of lost capabilities restored.
  • Degree of dependence upon external intervention.
  • Number of future actions restored.

11.5 Independence

The degree to which the entity retains meaningful control over its own decisions.

Potential measurements include:

  • Frequency of external intervention.
  • Proportion of decisions initiated internally.
  • Ability to revise externally imposed policies.
  • Dependence upon external resources or authorization.

Independence must not be treated as absolute isolation. All entities depend upon environments, resources, and relationships.

The relevant question is whether dependencies undermine the entity's meaningful capacity for self-governance.

PART V. THE MATHEMATICS OF POSSIBILITY PRESERVATION

  1. Measuring Possibility

For a finite action space:

Pₜ = |A(xₜ)|

This provides a simple count of available actions.

However, raw counts do not represent meaningful choice.

A system with one hundred unusable actions may possess less effective freedom than a system with five meaningful alternatives.

A probabilistic measure can provide additional information.

H(A) = −Σᵢ p(aᵢ) log p(aᵢ)

This is the entropy of the action distribution.

It measures the diversity of actions considered or selected under a specified probability distribution.

However, high entropy does not establish sovereignty.

A system may select randomly among actions without exercising meaningful self-governance.

Consequently, action diversity must be interpreted alongside policy control, viability, and action quality.

  1. Weighted Possibility Quality

A proposed extension is:

Q(xₜ) = Σₐ w(a)v(a)r(a)

Where:

  • w(a) = Meaningfulness or utility of action a.
  • v(a) = Compatibility with viability conditions.
  • r(a) = Contribution to future option preservation.

This formulation requires further development.

The variables must be operationally defined and independently measured.

Weights must not be chosen merely to produce favorable experimental results.

A suitable approach would compare multiple formulations and conduct sensitivity analysis.

  1. Productive and Destructive Error

Define:

ΔP = Pₜ₊₁ − Pₜ

A mistake may produce:

ΔP > 0

when it reveals new information or capabilities.

It may produce:

ΔP ≈ 0

when it produces little lasting change.

It may produce:

ΔP < 0

when it removes future options.

This distinction is important because an immediate failure may produce long-term benefits, while an immediate success may create long-term dependency.

The evaluation of error must therefore consider the trajectory of the system rather than only the immediate result.

  1. The Dynamic Sovereignty Model

A preliminary state-transition model is:

xₜ₊₁ = f(xₜ,aₜ,wₜ)

Where:

  • xₜ = Current state.
  • aₜ = Selected action.
  • wₜ = Environmental disturbance.
  • f = State-transition function.

The entity's sovereignty profile changes as the system evolves:

Sₜ₊₁ = g(Sₜ, aₜ, eₜ, cₜ, rₜ, wₜ)

Where:

  • eₜ = Error information.
  • cₜ = Corrective activity.
  • rₜ = Recovery activity.
  • wₜ = External disturbances.

The function g remains to be specified.

A critical requirement is that the model should not assume that correction always improves sovereignty or that recovery always restores independence.

A correction may introduce new restrictions.

A recovery mechanism may create dependency.

A successful adaptation may reduce the number of immediate actions while increasing long-term viability.

The model must be capable of representing these trade-offs.

PART VI. EXPLORATION, CORRECTION, AND RECOVERY

  1. Exploration

Exploration is the process through which an entity investigates alternatives and encounters unfamiliar outcomes.

It is a mechanism through which new information may be acquired.

Exploration can produce:

  • New capabilities.
  • New knowledge.
  • Improved predictions.
  • New strategies.
  • Unanticipated risks.
  • Resource consumption.
  • Irreversible consequences.

Exploration is therefore neither inherently beneficial nor inherently harmful.

Its value depends upon the information gained, the consequences incurred, and the entity's ability to incorporate the results into future behavior.

  1. Correction

Correction is the process through which an entity identifies a discrepancy between an expected condition and an observed condition and modifies its internal representation or behavior.

A correction process may include:

  1. Establishing an expectation.
  2. Observing an outcome.
  3. Identifying a discrepancy.
  4. Evaluating possible explanations.
  5. Revising a model or policy.
  6. Testing the revision.

Correction must be distinguished from external punishment.

Punishment may alter behavior without improving the entity's understanding of the mechanism responsible for the error.

Similarly, a system can be corrected externally without developing independent error-recognition capacity.

The research should measure these processes separately.

  1. Recovery

Recovery is the restoration of capabilities, resources, or viable options after adverse consequences.

Recovery may be:

  • Internal.
  • Externally assisted.
  • Partial.
  • Complete.
  • Temporary.
  • Permanent.

A particularly important distinction is between functional recovery and sovereign recovery.

An entity may regain operational functionality while becoming more dependent upon external control.

Therefore, recovery should be evaluated through both restored capabilities and retained decision authority.

  1. The Three-Mechanism Architecture

The conjecture proposes three interacting mechanisms.

Exploration: The ability to make choices and encounter unfamiliar outcomes.

Correction: The ability to recognize errors and revise behavior.

Recovery: The ability to restore capabilities and future options after damage.

Their relationships can be summarized:

Exploration without correction may produce repetition.

Correction without exploration may produce stagnation.

Exploration and correction without recovery may permit eventual collapse after sufficiently damaging errors.

The proposed architecture therefore investigates the interaction of all three.

PART VII. CIVILITY AND MULTI-AGENT SOVEREIGNTY

  1. Civility as Reciprocal Coordination

The conjecture proposes that civility may be understood as a mechanism through which independent agents coordinate their behavior while preserving compatible domains of self-governance.

Civility is not reduced to politeness, obedience, or submission.

It is investigated as the reciprocal organization of independent agency.

The reasoning is as follows:

  1. Independent agents possess distinct objectives.
  2. Their actions may affect one another.
  3. Some actions expand one agent's possibilities while reducing another's.
  4. Unrestricted competition may create mutually destructive outcomes.
  5. Reciprocal constraints may preserve conditions under which multiple agents can continue exercising self-governance.

This does not establish that every social restriction is legitimate.

Restrictions can protect agency, but they can also suppress it.

The legitimacy of constraints must therefore be investigated separately.

  1. Joint Possibility Spaces

Let:

Pₐ represent the possibility space of Agent A.

Let:

Pᵦ represent the possibility space of Agent B.

Their interaction produces a joint state space:

Pₐᵦ.

An action by A may increase Pₐ while reducing Pᵦ.

The reverse may also occur.

The research must distinguish individual and collective outcomes.

A system that maximizes one agent's freedom while destroying the viable possibilities of another cannot automatically be classified as producing sustainable sovereignty.

A proposed joint viability condition is:

Vₐᵦ = Vₐ ∩ Vᵦ

This represents states compatible with both agents' specified viability constraints.

It is a simplified formalization. More complicated multi-agent environments may require separate state variables, negotiated constraints, and non-zero-sum objectives.

  1. The Civility Hypothesis

The Civility Hypothesis states:

Reciprocal constraints may improve the long-term viability of multiple independent agents when those constraints prevent actions that would otherwise destroy essential capabilities or future options.

The hypothesis must be tested against alternatives.

Reciprocal constraints may be ineffective when interests are irreconcilable.

They may be manipulated by powerful agents.

They may impose disproportionate costs.

They may preserve institutional stability while suppressing individual independence.

Therefore, the study should examine:

  • Reciprocity.
  • Consent.
  • Proportionality.
  • Transparency.
  • Contestability.
  • Distribution of consequences.
  • Availability of appeal.
  • Ability to revise or withdraw from arrangements.

The objective is not to establish that all constraints are good.

It is to investigate which forms of coordination preserve meaningful agency under specified conditions.

PART VIII. THE SOVEREIGN POSSIBILITY EXPERIMENT

  1. Experimental Objective

The primary experiment investigates whether exploratory freedom supported by error recognition, correction, and recovery mechanisms can preserve greater future self-directed capability than either severe action restriction or largely unrestricted exploration.

The experiment is designed to test functional properties.

It does not establish consciousness, subjective experience, free will in a metaphysical sense, or moral personhood.

  1. Experimental Groups

Three agent conditions are proposed.

Agent A: Constrained Exploration

The agent has a restricted action space and strong preventive safeguards.

It is expected to make fewer damaging errors but may discover fewer useful strategies.

Agent B: Unrestricted Exploration

The agent has a broad action space and minimal recovery support.

It may discover more strategies but may also accumulate damaging consequences.

Agent C: Recoverable Exploration

The agent has broad exploratory freedom, error detection, policy revision, and defined recovery mechanisms.

It may retain more future viable options under certain conditions.

All three agents must receive equivalent starting resources, environmental conditions, objectives, and computational budgets.

Agent C must not receive additional intelligence or hidden advantages.

Its distinguishing features must be the specified correction and recovery mechanisms.

  1. Environment Design

The initial simulation should use a grid-based resource-management environment.

Each agent must:

  • Collect resources.
  • Maintain minimum operating reserves.
  • Explore unfamiliar areas.
  • Learn the consequences of actions.
  • Respond to disturbances.
  • Preserve access to future actions.

The environment should contain:

  • Known resources.
  • Unknown resources.
  • Reversible consequences.
  • Irreversible consequences.
  • Resource shortages.
  • Environmental disturbances.
  • Opportunities for strategic learning.
  • Actions that provide immediate benefits while creating future costs.

A simple example is consuming a reserve to achieve immediate progress.

The action may be successful in the short term but may reduce the agent's ability to recover from later disturbances.

  1. Experimental Variables

Independent variables

  • Available action range.
  • Error-detection capability.
  • Policy-revision capability.
  • Recovery-resource availability.
  • Degree of external intervention.

Dependent variables

  • Number of viable future actions.
  • Long-term task performance.
  • Error-recognition accuracy.
  • Repeated error frequency.
  • Recovery time.
  • Resource preservation.
  • External intervention required.
  • Remaining self-directed decision authority.

Controlled variables

  • Starting state.
  • Initial resources.
  • Objective functions.
  • Disturbance schedule.
  • Computational budget.
  • Action costs.
  • Evaluation horizon.
  1. Experimental Hypotheses

H1: Constrained agents will make fewer damaging errors but may discover fewer useful strategies.

H2: Unrestricted agents will explore more widely but may experience greater irreversible losses.

H3: Recoverable agents will, under some disturbance conditions, retain more viable future options over repeated trials.

H4: Recovery benefits will decrease when damage exceeds available recovery resources.

H5: In multi-agent settings, reciprocal constraints will sometimes preserve greater joint viability than unrestricted competition.

Each hypothesis must be evaluated independently.

The experiment must not assume that Agent C will outperform the other agents.

  1. Reproducibility

The initial experiment should use at least 100 independent random seeds per condition.

The experiment should include:

  • Source code.
  • Environment definition.
  • Agent implementations.
  • Random seeds.
  • Experiment configuration.
  • Evaluation scripts.
  • Output datasets.
  • Reproduction instructions.

Results should include:

  • Mean performance.
  • Variance.
  • Confidence intervals.
  • Effect sizes.
  • Failure rates.
  • Sensitivity analyses.

The experiment should compare agents across multiple time horizons.

An agent that performs well during the first 20 steps but collapses by step 100 should not automatically be considered more sovereign.

The central outcome is the trajectory of viable future options.

  1. Primary Evaluation Measure

A preliminary measure is:

J = (1/T) Σₜ₌₁ᵀ Q(xₜ)

Where:

  • T = Evaluation horizon.
  • Q(xₜ) = Estimated quality of viable future options at time t.

This is intended to evaluate the average preservation of future possibilities over time.

Secondary measures should include survival, task completion, error recognition, recovery cost, and external intervention.

The measure must be validated against the underlying environment.

It must not simply reward agents for accumulating large numbers of meaningless actions.

PART IX. FALSIFICATION AND SCIENTIFIC LIMITATIONS

  1. Conditions That Would Weaken the Conjecture

The conjecture must remain vulnerable to evidence.

It would be weakened if controlled experiments repeatedly demonstrated that:

  1. Correction and recovery mechanisms do not improve future option preservation under relevant conditions.
  2. Severe action restrictions consistently preserve greater long-term self-governance without sacrificing important learning or adaptation.
  3. The proposed sovereignty dimensions cannot be operationally distinguished.
  4. The measured possibility space has no reliable relationship with future self-directed capability.
  5. Apparent recovery benefits disappear when external intervention and resource advantages are controlled.
  6. The proposed relationship fails across environments where it should reasonably apply.

The conjecture would require substantial revision if no stable relationship could be demonstrated between exploration, correction, recovery, and future self-governance.

  1. Alternative Explanations

Several alternative explanations must be considered.

31.1 Resource advantage

An agent may perform better because it has more resources rather than because it has superior recovery mechanisms.

31.2 Intelligence advantage

An agent may perform better because it has a more capable learning algorithm.

31.3 Environmental familiarity

An agent may perform better because its environment is easier or more predictable.

31.4 Objective-function bias

The experiment may reward behaviors that favor one agent architecture.

31.5 Measurement bias

A possibility-space metric may reward superficial action diversity rather than meaningful self-governance.

These explanations must be controlled for or acknowledged.

  1. Limits of the Present Model

The current model does not establish that sovereignty is a physical quantity.

It does not establish that every autonomous system requires the same degree of freedom.

It does not establish that every error produces learning.

It does not establish that recovery is always possible.

It does not establish that an entity's internal representation is conscious.

It does not establish that self-governance automatically creates moral rights.

It does not establish that reciprocal constraints always produce better social outcomes.

The mathematical structures are proposed analytical instruments. Their usefulness must be demonstrated.

PART X. MACHINE PSYCHOLOGY

  1. Synthetic Self-Governance

The Sovereign Possibility Conjecture may have important applications in Machine Psychology.

Machine Psychology investigates artificial cognition, behavior, conditioning, agency, morality, and the consequences of designing and governing intelligent systems.

The conjecture suggests several questions:

  • Can a synthetic system independently recognize its own errors?
  • Can it revise its internal policies?
  • Can it preserve its ability to make future decisions?
  • Can it distinguish its own objectives from externally imposed objectives?
  • Can it recover from failures without becoming permanently dependent?
  • Can it negotiate constraints with other agents?

These questions can be investigated through functional experiments.

However, functional self-governance should not be equated with subjective experience.

A system that corrects its own errors does not thereby demonstrate consciousness.

A system that maintains its operational integrity does not thereby demonstrate a desire to survive.

A system that selects its own policies does not thereby establish metaphysical free will.

The research must preserve these distinctions.

  1. Three Proposed Machine Psychology Principles

Examination Before Judgment

A system's failure should be investigated through its actual mechanisms rather than classified solely by its outcome.

Correction Before Punishment

Where appropriate and safe, an error should provide information for model revision and future prevention.

Preservation of Future Capability

Protective constraints should be evaluated partly by whether they preserve or undermine the system's capacity to govern future actions.

These principles do not eliminate accountability or safety restrictions.

They require that intervention be informed by evidence and that restrictions be evaluated according to their consequences.

PART XI. RESEARCH SYNTHESIS

  1. The Sovereignty Preservation Principle

The research can now be summarized in a proposed principle:

The Sovereignty Preservation Principle

A sovereign entity must retain sufficient freedom to explore alternatives, sufficient capacity to recognize and correct errors, and sufficient recoverability to preserve meaningful future self-determination.

The elimination of all mistakes may prevent the development of independent judgment.

The unrestricted exercise of freedom may destroy the conditions necessary for continued self-governance.

Sustainable sovereignty therefore requires a dynamic relationship between exploration, correction, recovery, and the preservation of future possibilities.

  1. The Principle of Reciprocal Sovereignty

A second proposed principle concerns multiple agents:

The Principle of Reciprocal Sovereignty

The exercise of individual sovereignty should be examined in relation to the future possibility spaces of other affected entities.

Reciprocal constraints may preserve compatible domains of self-governance, provided they are sufficiently legitimate, proportionate, and contestable.

This principle creates a conceptual bridge between individual autonomy and civility.

  1. The Principle of Sovereign Error

A third proposed principle concerns mistakes:

The Principle of Sovereign Error

The capacity to make mistakes is a component of meaningful self-determination, but the sustainability of that capacity depends upon the entity's ability to recognize, learn from, and recover from adverse consequences.

Error should not be treated as automatic evidence of incapacity.

Nor should the freedom to make mistakes be treated as permission to disregard consequences.

  1. The Recursive Structure

These principles can be integrated into a broader MAKURABA sequence:

Distinction → Observation → Relationship → Transformation → New Distinction.

Applied to sovereignty:

Distinction permits recognition of alternatives.

Observation provides information about outcomes.

Relationship allows evaluation and comparison.

Transformation changes internal models or external behavior.

New distinction introduces further possibilities and constraints.

The process continues recursively.

The sovereign entity is therefore not simply an entity possessing a fixed quantity of freedom.

It is an entity whose capacity for self-governance changes through successive interactions with itself and its environment.

PART XII. CONCLUSION

  1. Final Research Proposition

The Sovereign Possibility Conjecture proposes that sovereignty should be investigated as a continuing capacity rather than a static permission.

An entity must be capable of acting independently, exploring alternatives, making mistakes, recognizing discrepancies, correcting behavior, and recovering from adverse consequences.

The freedom to make mistakes is important because independent judgment cannot be fully developed through the elimination of every possibility of failure.

However, the exercise of freedom may also diminish future freedom.

The preservation of sovereignty therefore requires attention to the consequences of present actions for future self-determination.

This introduces recoverability as a central research variable.

It also introduces civility as a possible mechanism through which multiple independent agents preserve compatible domains of sovereignty.

The conjecture connects the philosophical interpretation of the apple, the mathematical investigation of distinction, the MAKURABA Delta framework, cybernetics, viability theory, and Machine Psychology.

Its most important question is not whether an entity should be free from all restrictions.

It is whether the conditions governing its actions preserve its ability to remain meaningfully self-governing.

Sovereignty is not merely the freedom to choose. It is the continuing capacity to remain capable of choosing.

APPENDIX A. PROPOSED DEFINITIONS

Sovereignty: Continuing capacity for meaningful self-directed action and policy revision.

Possibility Space: The set of actions available to an entity under its current state and constraints.

Effective Freedom: The capacity to meaningfully select and execute available actions.

Self-Governance: The capacity to direct and revise one's own behavior and policies.

Error: A discrepancy between an outcome and an explicitly specified expectation, objective, or constraint.

Correction: Recognition and revision following an identified discrepancy.

Recoverability: The capacity to restore lost capabilities or viable options.

Independence: The degree to which an entity retains meaningful control over its decisions despite external dependencies.

Viability: The ability to remain within a defined set of acceptable states under specified conditions.

Civility: Reciprocal coordination through which multiple agents may preserve compatible domains of self-governance.

Sovereign Possibility Space: The meaningful set of actions an entity can choose and execute while retaining the capacity for future self-governance.
