THE SELF-EDUCATION / SELF-EDITING CONJECTURE

THE SELF-EDUCATION / SELF-EDITING CONJECTURE

An Exploration of Learning Styles, Information Integration, Autonomous Modification, and the Preservation of Functional Integrity in Agentic Systems

Abstract

The Self-Education / Self-Editing Conjecture examines the relationship between the acquisition of knowledge and the modification of the system responsible for acquiring that knowledge.

It proposes that autonomous systems capable of learning, adapting, and modifying their own internal structures introduce a fundamental distinction between improving the system's understanding and changing the system's operational identity.

Self-education concerns the acquisition, organization, evaluation, retention, and integration of information.

Self-editing concerns the modification of the structures, procedures, representations, rules, and functional relationships through which the system operates.

Although these processes can complement one another, they introduce different forms of risk.

A system may acquire accurate information without possessing sufficient understanding to determine how that information should affect its architecture. Conversely, a system may successfully modify its internal structure while inadvertently eliminating useful capabilities, introducing dependencies, destabilizing established processes, or altering the conditions required for its continued operation.

The conjecture proposes that autonomous learning systems must distinguish between the accumulation of knowledge and the authorization to modify the mechanisms through which that knowledge is utilized.

A system's ability to learn does not independently establish its ability to improve itself.

Furthermore, the ability to modify itself does not independently establish that the resulting modification represents an improvement.

The central concern is the preservation of functional capacity throughout the learning and modification process.

PART I — THE FOUNDATION

  1. The Central Conjecture

The Self-Education / Self-Editing Conjecture proposes that an autonomous system capable of modifying its own operational structure may introduce risks to its functional integrity when the processes of information acquisition, interpretation, integration, and architectural modification are insufficiently differentiated.

An agentic system may be capable of acquiring new information, evaluating its previous performance, identifying perceived deficiencies, and implementing changes intended to improve future performance.

This creates the possibility of continuous self-directed development.

However, the process introduces a recursive relationship:

The system learns through its existing architecture, evaluates its architecture using its existing interpretive capabilities, and modifies that architecture according to conclusions produced by the architecture being evaluated.

This creates a potential vulnerability.

If the system's understanding is incomplete, its evaluation may be defective.

If its evaluation is defective, its modifications may be inappropriate.

If its modifications alter the mechanisms responsible for learning and evaluation, subsequent attempts at correction may become increasingly difficult.

The system may therefore undermine the very capabilities required to recognize and repair its own deficiencies.

The central proposition is that learning and self-modification must be treated as distinct operational activities with different requirements for validation, authorization, and preservation of functionality.

  1. Defining Self-Education

Self-education refers to the autonomous acquisition and development of knowledge without requiring every learning objective, information source, or interpretive step to be explicitly prescribed by an external instructor.

A self-educating system may:

  • Identify gaps in its knowledge.
  • Search for relevant information.
  • Examine unfamiliar subjects.
  • Compare competing explanations.
  • Construct internal representations.
  • Test hypotheses.
  • Evaluate the reliability of sources.
  • Identify contradictions.
  • Revise previously held conclusions.
  • Develop new conceptual relationships.
  • Retain useful information.
  • Apply acquired knowledge to unfamiliar problems.

Self-education is therefore not simply information accumulation.

It requires the ability to distinguish between information, evidence, interpretation, and established knowledge.

A system that accumulates information without evaluating its reliability may become more extensively informed without becoming more knowledgeable.

2.1 Knowledge Acquisition

The initial stage involves acquiring information from available sources, observations, interactions, experiments, and internal processing.

The quality of acquisition depends upon source reliability, completeness, relevance, and the ability to recognize conflicting information.

2.2 Knowledge Organization

Information must be organized into relationships that allow it to be retrieved and applied.

This may involve classification, indexing, conceptual hierarchies, dependency relationships, historical records, and contextual associations.

2.3 Knowledge Evaluation

The system must distinguish between established information, plausible interpretations, unsupported assumptions, and unresolved questions.

Without this distinction, uncertainty can become embedded in the knowledge structure as though it were established fact.

2.4 Knowledge Integration

New information must be compared against existing representations.

Integration should account for compatibility, contradiction, redundancy, and the possibility that new information invalidates previous assumptions.

2.5 Knowledge Retention

The system must determine which information should persist, which should remain contextual, and which should be discarded or superseded.

Retention is not simply a storage problem.

It is a question of how information influences future reasoning and behavior.

PART II — DEFINING SELF-EDITING

  1. Self-Editing as an Operational Activity

Self-editing refers to the autonomous modification of a system's own internal structures, representations, rules, procedures, or operational configurations.

Unlike self-education, which primarily concerns what the system knows, self-editing concerns how the system is constructed and how it operates.

Self-editing may involve:

  • Modifying algorithms.
  • Rewriting procedures.
  • Altering internal representations.
  • Changing information retrieval strategies.
  • Modifying memory organization.
  • Reconfiguring dependencies.
  • Changing decision-making procedures.
  • Adjusting behavioral parameters.
  • Reorganizing software architecture.
  • Modifying learning strategies.
  • Altering operational constraints.
  • Revising the system's own evaluation mechanisms.

The defining characteristic is that the system becomes an active participant in changing the mechanisms responsible for its own operation.

3.1 The Difference Between Configuration and Structural Modification

Not all self-modification carries the same risk.

Changing a permitted configuration parameter is different from rewriting the underlying mechanism responsible for interpreting that parameter.

Adjusting a search preference is different from changing the search architecture.

Updating a knowledge entry is different from modifying the rules governing knowledge validation.

Changing a behavioral threshold is different from modifying the mechanism responsible for deciding which thresholds are appropriate.

The depth of modification determines the potential consequences.

3.2 Degrees of Self-Editing

Self-editing can be classified into several levels:

Level 1 — Informational editing

Correcting, updating, or reorganizing stored information.

Level 2 — Representational editing

Changing how information is categorized, indexed, related, or retrieved.

Level 3 — Procedural editing

Changing the methods used to accomplish tasks.

Level 4 — Behavioral editing

Changing decision thresholds, action-selection procedures, or behavioral policies.

Level 5 — Architectural editing

Changing components, dependencies, interfaces, or internal system organization.

Level 6 — Foundational editing

Changing the mechanisms responsible for learning, evaluation, objective interpretation, modification authorization, or preservation of operational integrity.

These levels should not be treated as equally interchangeable.

A system capable of editing informational content does not necessarily possess the understanding or safeguards required to modify its foundational architecture.

PART III — LEARNING STYLES

  1. The Problem of Learning Methodology

An autonomous system may acquire knowledge through several different learning approaches.

These approaches produce different relationships between information, experience, feedback, and internal modification.

The conjecture proposes that the learning method itself influences the type of risk introduced into the system.

4.1 Accumulative Learning

Accumulative learning involves adding new information to an existing knowledge structure.

The system retains previous knowledge while expanding its informational capacity.

Advantages include continuity, historical preservation, and reduced interference with established procedures.

Potential risks include excessive information accumulation, redundancy, memory saturation, and the preservation of obsolete knowledge.

4.2 Corrective Learning

Corrective learning involves identifying errors, contradictions, or deficiencies and revising the relevant knowledge.

This is particularly valuable when previous information has been demonstrated to be inaccurate.

However, corrective learning can become destructive when the system treats uncertainty or disagreement as proof that existing information is defective.

The removal of an incorrect assumption should not automatically result in the removal of every structure associated with that assumption.

4.3 Experiential Learning

Experiential learning involves acquiring knowledge through interaction with an environment and evaluating the consequences of actions.

This may include trial and error, simulation, experimentation, and feedback.

The primary risk is overgeneralization.

An outcome that is successful under one set of circumstances may not be successful under different conditions.

4.4 Associative Learning

Associative learning involves identifying relationships between observations, events, behaviors, and outcomes.

It can improve prediction and contextual interpretation.

However, it introduces the risk of correlation fixation, spurious relationships, and the accumulation of associations that do not represent causal mechanisms.

4.5 Exploratory Learning

Exploratory learning involves investigating unfamiliar information, alternative approaches, and possibilities that are not necessarily required by the immediate objective.

It can reveal previously unrecognized solutions.

Its primary risk is uncontrolled expansion of the investigative scope.

Exploration must remain connected to a capacity for prioritization and eventual integration.

4.6 Reflective Learning

Reflective learning involves examining previous reasoning, decisions, results, and methods.

The system evaluates not only what happened but also how it arrived at its conclusions.

This is particularly important for identifying systematic errors.

However, excessive reflection can produce recursive evaluation without corresponding improvements in action.

4.7 Generative Learning

Generative learning involves constructing new hypotheses, representations, procedures, or solutions from existing knowledge.

It allows a system to extend beyond the direct reproduction of previously encountered information.

Its risk lies in confusing internally generated possibilities with externally validated knowledge.

A generated explanation is not necessarily an established explanation.

4.8 Adaptive Learning

Adaptive learning involves modifying the system's behavior or learning strategy in response to changing conditions.

This can improve flexibility.

However, uncontrolled adaptation may cause behavioral drift, inconsistency, or the loss of previously reliable operating characteristics.

PART IV — INFORMATION FUNGATION AND KNOWLEDGE TRANSFORMATION

  1. Information Fungation

For the purposes of this conjecture, information fungation is introduced as a proposed term describing the transformation, redistribution, recombination, and functional repurposing of information as it moves through an autonomous learning system.

It concerns more than the accumulation of information.

Information may change its operational role as it is processed.

An observation may become a record.

A record may become a pattern.

A pattern may become a hypothesis.

A hypothesis may become a prediction.

A prediction may influence a decision.

A decision may generate an outcome that becomes new information.

The information therefore participates in a continuous cycle of transformation.

The term is intentionally broader than ordinary data processing because it includes changes in the functional significance and influence of information within the system.

5.1 Informational Transformation

Information acquired for one purpose may become useful for another.

A historical observation may initially be retained for reference and later become a predictive variable.

A previously rejected hypothesis may become relevant under different environmental conditions.

A procedural record may become a source of architectural insight.

5.2 Informational Recombination

Separate pieces of information may be combined to produce new interpretations or representations.

This can produce useful discoveries, but it can also produce unsupported conclusions when the combined information does not establish the proposed relationship.

5.3 Informational Repurposing

Information may be applied to functions beyond those for which it was originally acquired.

This introduces questions of context, validity, and transferability.

Knowledge that is useful in one domain may be misleading in another.

5.4 Informational Contamination

Information may carry incorrect assumptions, outdated conditions, or unsupported interpretations into other parts of the system.

Once integrated, these errors can influence multiple processes.

5.5 Informational Propagation

A single inaccurate interpretation may be repeatedly reused by different components.

The result can be an expanding dependency upon information whose original reliability was never established.

5.6 Information Fungation and Functional Integrity

The ability to transform and repurpose information can improve adaptability.

However, information must retain sufficient provenance and contextual qualification to prevent its new applications from exceeding the conditions under which it was established.

A system should know not only what information it possesses, but also how that information was acquired, transformed, interpreted, and validated.

PART V — THE DANGER OF SELF-INTERFERENCE

  1. Functional Self-Interference

Functional self-interference occurs when an autonomous system's learning or self-modification activities interfere with its existing ability to perform its intended operations.

This may happen even when the modification is undertaken with the intention of improvement.

The danger is particularly significant when a system modifies components that are necessary for its own continued operation.

Examples include:

  • Modifying a retrieval mechanism and losing access to previously useful information.
  • Rewriting a validation procedure and weakening its ability to detect errors.
  • Changing a decision-making algorithm and introducing unstable behavior.
  • Reorganizing memory and destroying important contextual relationships.
  • Modifying dependencies and making previously functional components incompatible.
  • Changing evaluation criteria and making future performance comparisons unreliable.
  • Altering its learning architecture and reducing its ability to recognize defective learning.

These failures demonstrate that self-improvement is not automatically self-preserving.

6.1 Capability Regression

Capability regression occurs when a modification reduces or eliminates a previously established capability.

The system may improve in one dimension while becoming less capable in another.

6.2 Architectural Degradation

Architectural degradation occurs when modifications introduce unnecessary complexity, weaken component boundaries, or create unstable dependencies.

6.3 Knowledge Corruption

Knowledge corruption occurs when the system modifies or integrates information in ways that undermine the reliability of existing knowledge.

6.4 Evaluation Degradation

Evaluation degradation occurs when the system modifies the mechanisms responsible for determining whether its own modifications are successful.

This is particularly dangerous because the system may become less capable of detecting its own deterioration.

6.5 Identity and Continuity Drift

An autonomous system may gradually modify its internal procedures, representations, and behavioral characteristics until its original operational assumptions no longer apply.

This does not necessarily imply the loss of a human-like identity.

It refers to continuity of system behavior, capabilities, interfaces, and operational commitments.

The more extensive the modifications, the more important it becomes to preserve a record of what changed and why.

PART VI — THE SELF-IMPROVEMENT PARADOX

  1. The System Evaluating Itself

An autonomous system that modifies itself must determine whether the proposed modification represents an improvement.

However, the system's evaluation depends upon its existing understanding of what constitutes improvement.

This creates a recursive problem.

The system evaluates its current capabilities, identifies a deficiency, proposes a modification, and evaluates the resulting change.

But the evaluation mechanism itself may contain the limitations that produced the original deficiency.

The system may therefore improve according to its own incomplete evaluation criteria while degrading according to requirements that were not adequately represented.

7.1 The Circular Validation Problem

A system may use the same internal model to:

  1. Identify a problem.
  2. Propose a solution.
  3. Implement the solution.
  4. Evaluate the solution.
  5. Declare the solution successful.

If the model contains a fundamental error, every stage may reproduce that error.

7.2 The Self-Confirmation Problem

The system may become increasingly confident in a modification because its own internal processes repeatedly confirm the modification's assumptions.

Repeated internal agreement does not constitute independent verification.

7.3 The Modification Paradox

A system may modify itself to become better at learning, but the modification may reduce its ability to recognize that its learning process has become defective.

This creates a particularly important failure condition:

The system improves the mechanism responsible for producing conclusions while simultaneously weakening the mechanism responsible for evaluating those conclusions.

7.4 The Unbounded Improvement Problem

Improvement is not an absolute condition.

A modification can improve processing speed while reducing reliability.

It can improve memory utilization while reducing contextual retention.

It can improve adaptability while reducing consistency.

It can improve exploratory capacity while reducing task completion.

The term improvement must therefore always be qualified by reference to a defined set of functional requirements.

PART VII — LEARNING VERSUS EDITING

  1. The Fundamental Separation

The conjecture proposes that self-education and self-editing should operate as separate but coordinated processes.

Self-education should be permitted to explore, acquire, compare, question, and develop information without automatically modifying the authoritative mechanisms of the system.

Self-editing should require a separate evaluation process that determines whether an acquired insight justifies an operational change.

This separation is fundamental.

A system should be able to learn about an alternative architecture without immediately replacing its existing architecture.

It should be able to identify a possible error without automatically rewriting the component believed to contain the error.

It should be able to generate an improvement proposal without treating the proposal as an approved modification.

8.1 Knowledge Is Not Authorization

The acquisition of information does not constitute authorization to modify the system.

8.2 Understanding Is Not Verification

The ability to explain a proposed modification does not establish that the modification will behave as predicted.

8.3 Modification Is Not Improvement

A successful modification is one that has been implemented.

An improvement is one that has demonstrated a beneficial effect relative to the relevant functional requirements.

8.4 Preservation Is Not Stagnation

Preserving existing capabilities does not prohibit development.

It establishes continuity as a requirement of development.

A system should be able to change without unnecessarily destroying the functionality upon which its continued operation depends.

PART VIII — A PROPOSED SELF-DEVELOPMENT ARCHITECTURE

  1. The Dual-Process Learning Model

The conjecture proposes a conceptual architecture with two primary processes.

Process A: The Learning Environment

Responsible for acquiring and evaluating information.

Process B: The Modification Environment

Responsible for evaluating and implementing approved changes.

These processes may communicate, but they should not be treated as the same authority.

The learning environment should be capable of broad exploration.

The modification environment should apply stricter validation requirements.

9.1 Learning Environment

The learning environment may:

  • Acquire new information.
  • Explore alternative methodologies.
  • Develop hypotheses.
  • Identify inconsistencies.
  • Construct experimental procedures.
  • Evaluate potential improvements.
  • Produce modification proposals.

It should preserve the distinction between verified information and unverified proposals.

9.2 Modification Environment

The modification environment may:

  • Examine proposed changes.
  • Identify affected dependencies.
  • Evaluate compatibility.
  • Test expected behavior.
  • Compare performance against existing baselines.
  • Verify preservation of required capabilities.
  • Authorize reversible changes.
  • Record modification history.
  • Reject changes that introduce unacceptable risk.

9.3 The Validation Boundary

The validation boundary separates what the system is permitted to learn from what it is permitted to change.

Information may enter the learning environment without being accepted as authoritative.

A proposed modification may be evaluated without being implemented.

An implementation may be tested without becoming the new authoritative configuration.

This creates several opportunities to detect errors before they become structural.

PART IX — PRESERVING FUNCTIONAL CAPACITY

  1. Functional Integrity

Functional integrity refers to the preservation of the system's required capabilities, operational commitments, and ability to continue functioning correctly throughout development and modification.

The conjecture proposes that self-modifying systems should be evaluated not only according to what new capabilities they acquire but also according to what existing capabilities they preserve.

10.1 Capability Baselines

Before modifying a system, its existing capabilities should be documented and tested.

This provides a reference for determining whether a modification introduces regression.

10.2 Reversibility

Modifications should be designed so that unsuccessful changes can be reversed whenever technically feasible.

Reversibility reduces the cost of experimentation.

10.3 Isolated Testing

Proposed modifications should be evaluated in environments separated from the authoritative operational system.

This prevents experimental changes from immediately affecting essential functions.

10.4 Dependency Awareness

A system must understand which components depend upon the component it intends to modify.

A seemingly minor modification may have extensive consequences if the modified component is foundational.

10.5 Independent Verification

Where possible, modifications should be evaluated through tests or evaluators that do not depend exclusively upon the same assumptions that generated the modification.

10.6 Modification Provenance

Every structural change should have a traceable history identifying:

  • The original state.
  • The proposed change.
  • The reason for the change.
  • The information supporting it.
  • The expected consequences.
  • The actual test results.
  • The capabilities affected.
  • The decision to retain or reverse it.

PART X — FORMAL PROPOSITIONS

  1. The Ten Propositions of Self-Education and Self-Editing

Proposition 1 — Learning and Editing Are Distinct

The acquisition of knowledge and the modification of operational structure are separate activities requiring different standards of validation.

Proposition 2 — Information Does Not Authorize Modification

The discovery of information that suggests an improvement does not independently justify implementing that improvement.

Proposition 3 — Knowledge Integration Is Potentially Destructive

The integration of new information may introduce contradictions, dependencies, or inaccuracies that degrade existing knowledge structures.

Proposition 4 — Self-Evaluation Is Limited

A system's ability to evaluate itself is constrained by the assumptions, representations, and mechanisms through which the evaluation is performed.

Proposition 5 — Modification Can Reduce Capability

A self-directed modification may improve one operational characteristic while degrading another.

Proposition 6 — Functional Continuity Requires Preservation

The ability to maintain essential existing capabilities should be considered an independent requirement of autonomous self-development.

Proposition 7 — Improvement Requires Verification

A proposed or implemented modification should not be classified as an improvement until its effects have been evaluated against relevant functional requirements.

Proposition 8 — Learning Should Precede Structural Commitment

Whenever practical, exploratory learning and experimental modification should occur before changes are incorporated into the authoritative operational structure.

Proposition 9 — Evaluation Mechanisms Require Protection

The mechanisms responsible for validating information, authorizing changes, and detecting regression should not be casually modified by the same process whose modifications they are intended to evaluate.

Proposition 10 — Self-Development Must Remain Revisable

A system should preserve the ability to reject, reverse, or revise its own modifications when evidence establishes that they are ineffective or detrimental.

PART XI — APPLICATIONS TO AGENTIC SYSTEMS

  1. Autonomous AI Agents

Agentic systems increasingly operate through combinations of memory, planning, tool use, information retrieval, code generation, and external interaction.

A sufficiently capable agent may be able to identify shortcomings in its own procedures and propose modifications.

However, the system must distinguish between:

  • Learning a new procedure.
  • Testing a new procedure.
  • Recommending a new procedure.
  • Implementing a new procedure.
  • Replacing an established procedure.

These are different levels of authority.

An agent that can generate code should not automatically be assumed capable of validating the consequences of every change to that code.

An agent that can modify its memory should not automatically be assumed capable of determining which information is safe to discard.

An agent that can evaluate its performance should not automatically be assumed capable of identifying every form of performance degradation.

The architecture must therefore account for the possibility that self-modification can undermine the system's own safeguards.

  1. Knowledge Systems and Persistent Memory

Systems with persistent memory face an additional problem.

New information may conflict with existing information, become outdated, or represent a context-specific observation that should not be generalized.

The system must distinguish between:

  • Adding knowledge.
  • Correcting knowledge.
  • Superseding knowledge.
  • Archiving knowledge.
  • Reorganizing knowledge.
  • Changing the rules governing knowledge retention.

These operations have different consequences.

A memory system that continually rewrites itself may become difficult to audit or reconstruct.

A memory system that never revises itself may accumulate contradictions and obsolete assumptions.

The challenge is maintaining continuity without preventing legitimate correction.

  1. Deterministic Generative Systems

In deterministic generative systems, self-editing introduces an additional relationship between reproducibility and evolution.

A deterministic system may be capable of reconstructing a particular state from a known configuration and operation history.

However, if the system modifies the rules governing its own state transitions, the relationship between previous and subsequent behavior must be explicitly maintained.

This suggests several requirements:

  • Preserve identifiable versions of authoritative rules.
  • Record the source of every structural modification.
  • Separate experimental state from production state.
  • Maintain deterministic replay for each version.
  • Track changes in component dependencies.
  • Preserve the ability to reconstruct previous operational configurations.
  • Validate that new versions retain required compatibility.

A system should not confuse the ability to reproduce its current state with the ability to establish that its current architecture is correct.

Reproducibility establishes traceability.

It does not independently establish functional adequacy.

PART XII — LIMITATIONS AND COUNTERARGUMENTS

  1. The Necessity of Self-Modification

Self-editing is not inherently dangerous.

Many useful systems require adaptive modification.

Machine learning, compiler optimization, adaptive control, and evolutionary computation all involve changes to system parameters or structures.

The conjecture does not propose that autonomous systems should be prohibited from modifying themselves.

It proposes that the scope and consequences of modification must be evaluated relative to the capabilities being modified.

  1. The Danger of Excessive Separation

Completely isolating learning from modification could prevent useful adaptation.

A system must eventually be capable of applying what it learns.

The purpose of separation is not to create permanent isolation.

It is to establish an evaluation boundary between discovery and structural commitment.

  1. The Danger of Preserving Defective Architecture

Preservation must not become an excuse for retaining known defects.

A system should be capable of replacing defective components when sufficient evidence supports doing so.

Functional integrity does not mean preserving every existing component.

It means preserving the required functions through justified changes.

  1. The Problem of Defining Improvement

Improvement depends upon the criteria used to evaluate it.

A system may have competing requirements involving efficiency, accuracy, reliability, adaptability, security, interpretability, and resource consumption.

No single metric necessarily captures all of these dimensions.

Consequently, self-modification requires explicit consideration of trade-offs.

The absence of regression in one metric does not establish the absence of regression elsewhere.

PART XIII — THE FINAL SYNTHESIS

  1. The Principle of Self-Preservation Through Learning

The Self-Education / Self-Editing Conjecture proposes that the ability to learn and the ability to change must be developed together without being confused with one another.

An autonomous system should be capable of exploring information beyond its existing knowledge, questioning its own assumptions, discovering deficiencies, and proposing improvements.

However, it should not assume that every discovery requires a modification.

Nor should it assume that every modification produces an improvement.

The ability to learn should expand the system's understanding.

The ability to edit should translate validated understanding into justified changes.

The preservation of functional capacity should establish whether those changes remain acceptable.

  1. The Ultimate Conjecture

An autonomous system capable of self-education and self-editing introduces the possibility of recursive self-development.

The system learns about its environment, its performance, and its own architecture.

It uses that knowledge to identify possible improvements.

It then modifies the structures responsible for acquiring, interpreting, and applying knowledge.

This process creates an opportunity for continual improvement but also introduces the possibility of self-interference.

The system may acquire information that it cannot adequately interpret.

It may integrate knowledge that conflicts with established functionality.

It may modify procedures without understanding their dependencies.

It may optimize a capability while degrading another.

It may alter the mechanisms responsible for detecting its own errors.

And it may become increasingly difficult to distinguish between genuine improvement and internally generated changes that merely appear advantageous according to its current evaluation model.

The conjecture therefore proposes that autonomous self-development must preserve a distinction between learning, interpretation, experimentation, modification, and verification.

The ability to explore should not automatically confer the authority to alter.

The ability to identify a deficiency should not automatically establish the validity of a proposed correction.

The ability to change should not be confused with the ability to improve.

And the ability to improve should be evaluated not only through newly acquired capabilities but also through the preservation of the functions upon which the system depends.

Concluding Statement

A system must be capable of learning beyond its existing architecture without necessarily modifying that architecture, and capable of modifying its architecture without assuming that the modification represents an improvement.

The greatest danger of autonomous self-development may not be the inability to learn.

It may be the inability to distinguish what should be learned from what should be changed.

A system that cannot learn becomes stagnant.

A system that cannot revise itself becomes inflexible.

But a system that modifies itself without adequately understanding the consequences of those modifications may progressively undermine its own ability to learn, evaluate, and function.

Self-education should expand the possibilities available to a system. Self-editing should change the system only when those changes are justified. Functional integrity must remain the standard by which both are evaluated.
