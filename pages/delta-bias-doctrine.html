DELTA BIAS DOCTRINE

THE DELTA BIAS DOCTRINE

Observational Integrity, Determinational Independence, and Corrective Revision

Foundational Doctrine

  1. Purpose

The Delta Bias Doctrine establishes the principles governing observational integrity and the prevention of interpretive distortion within Delta.

Its purpose is to ensure that an observer's reference point, expectations, assumptions, and subjective preferences do not become indistinguishable from the conditions being examined.

Observation is not inherently objective merely because it is deliberate. An observer may accurately record an occurrence while incorrectly interpreting its meaning, assigning inappropriate significance, or drawing conclusions unsupported by the available information.

The doctrine therefore establishes that every observation, interpretation, and determination must remain subject to examination, contradiction, and revision.

The objective is not to eliminate the observer's perspective, but to prevent that perspective from becoming an unquestionable authority over the observed conditions.

  1. The Principle of Observational Independence

An observation must be distinguishable from the interpretation assigned to it.

The observer must maintain a separation between what has been directly observed, what has been inferred, what has been assumed, and what remains unknown.

An interpretation does not become an established condition simply because it is consistent with the observer's existing understanding.

Likewise, an observation does not automatically validate the explanation attached to it.

The validity of an interpretation must be established through its relationship with available evidence rather than its compatibility with existing beliefs.

  1. The Principle of Reference-Point Fallibility

Every reference point is subject to potential error.

An observer's current reference may be incomplete, outdated, improperly constructed, or influenced by conditions that distort its interpretation.

No reference point shall be considered permanently authoritative.

The observer must retain the capacity to revise their reference when new information, contradictory observations, or previously unidentified conditions become available.

A change in reference must not automatically be interpreted as a failure of observation. It may represent the successful correction of an earlier limitation.

  1. The Principle of Weighting Independence

The relative significance assigned to observed conditions must remain open to reconsideration.

An observer may assign excessive weight to familiar, emotionally significant, or personally convenient conditions while neglecting influences that are less immediately apparent.

Such weighting may distort the interpretation of the observer's current state and the assessment of available trajectories.

Influence weights must therefore be justified by identifiable relevance, supporting evidence, and their relationship to the determination being examined.

No condition shall receive permanent significance solely because it has previously been assigned importance.

  1. The Principle of Boundary Examination

An assumed boundary must not be confused with an established limitation.

Observers may incorrectly identify a condition as impossible, inaccessible, permanent, or beyond their influence when the apparent limitation is instead conditional, temporary, misunderstood, or insufficiently examined.

All significant boundaries should be classified according to the available evidence supporting their existence.

The observer must distinguish between:

  • Demonstrated limitations.
  • Conditional limitations.
  • Resource-dependent limitations.
  • Unverified limitations.
  • Perceived limitations.
  • Unknown boundaries.

The absence of an identified pathway does not establish the impossibility of that pathway.

  1. The Principle of Contradictory Observation

Contradictory evidence must be examined rather than dismissed solely because it conflicts with an existing determination.

An observer must remain capable of identifying information that weakens, challenges, or invalidates their current interpretation.

Evidence must not be selectively accepted according to whether it supports a preferred conclusion.

When contradictory observations emerge, the observer must examine whether the discrepancy originates from incomplete information, an incorrect reference point, inappropriate weighting, changing conditions, or an invalid interpretation.

Contradiction is not inherently a failure of the observational process.

It is an opportunity for correction.

  1. The Principle of Determinational Independence

A determination must be independently justified by the observations and reasoning supporting it.

The observer must not convert an assumption into a determination without establishing the relationship between the assumption and the available evidence.

Determinations must remain distinguishable from predictions, expectations, preferences, and intentions.

A conclusion may be logically consistent while remaining factually incorrect.

Accordingly, internal consistency alone is insufficient to establish validity.

Every significant determination must remain open to external examination and possible invalidation.

  1. The Principle of Counterfactual Examination

The observer must periodically examine alternative explanations for significant determinations.

This requires considering what conditions, observations, or information would demonstrate that the current interpretation is incorrect.

The observer should actively seek plausible alternatives rather than restrict examination to explanations that preserve the existing reference point.

A determination becomes more defensible when it has survived reasonable attempts to challenge it.

The inability to identify an alternative explanation does not, by itself, establish certainty.

  1. The Principle of Provisional Confidence

Determinations must reflect the strength and limitations of their supporting observations.

The observer should distinguish between:

Provisional: A determination supported by incomplete or preliminary information.

Contested: A determination challenged by contradictory observations or competing interpretations.

Supported: A determination consistent with the available evidence and surviving reasonable examination.

Invalidated: A determination contradicted by sufficient evidence or shown to rely upon an incorrect premise.

These classifications describe the current evidential standing of a determination, not its permanent status.

A supported determination may become contested or invalidated when new information emerges.

Likewise, a provisional determination may become better supported through additional observation.

  1. The Principle of Corrective Revision

When an observational or interpretive error is identified, the observer must permit the affected determination to be revised.

Corrections should identify the nature of the error, the conditions responsible for it, and the resulting changes to the observer's understanding.

Previous conclusions must not be preserved merely to maintain consistency with earlier commitments.

Revision is not an admission of intellectual failure.

It is an essential component of observational integrity.

The observer must prioritize the correction of an inaccurate determination over the preservation of personal certainty.

  1. The Principle of Non-Exemption

The observer is not exempt from the examination being conducted.

The observer's motivations, expectations, assumptions, interpretive methods, and assigned weights may themselves become sources of distortion.

No determination shall be considered protected from examination by virtue of its origin, importance, emotional significance, or previous acceptance.

The methods used to evaluate an observation must also remain subject to evaluation.

  1. Foundational Doctrine

Delta requires the separation of observation from interpretation, interpretation from determination, and determination from obligation.

No assumption shall acquire the status of an established condition merely through repetition or acceptance.

No reference point shall be permanently privileged.

No assigned weight shall be immune to reconsideration.

No perceived boundary shall be treated as absolute without sufficient examination.

Contradictory evidence must remain admissible, and every determination must retain the possibility of revision.

The observer must be willing to discover that their original understanding was incorrect.

The purpose of observation is not to confirm the observer. It is to examine the observed.

The purpose of determination is not to preserve certainty. It is to establish the most defensible conclusion available.

The purpose of correction is not to protect the original trajectory. It is to permit a more accurate one.

Governing Maxim

NO OBSERVATION IS EXEMPT FROM EXAMINATION.

NO DETERMINATION IS EXEMPT FROM REVISION.

NO OBSERVER IS EXEMPT FROM ERROR.
