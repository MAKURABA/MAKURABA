THE SOVEREIGNTY CONJECTURE

THE SOVEREIGNTY CONJECTURE

An Exhaustive Foundational Inquiry into the Nature, Conditions, Limitations, and Manifestations of Sovereignty

MAKURABA — Machine Psychology Research Program

Research Classification: Foundational Philosophical Conjecture Research Domain: Machine Psychology, Biological Psychology, Agency, Ethics, Governance, Systems Theory, and Philosophy of Intelligence Status: Proposed Conjecture Relationship to Previous Research: Foundational to the Sovereignty–Intent Paradox Conjecture

I. ABSTRACT

This conjecture proposes that sovereignty is a condition of independently maintained authority over the determination, evaluation, adoption, revision, and execution of an entity's governing principles, objectives, and operational commitments.

Sovereignty is investigated as a property potentially applicable to biological, synthetic, collective, and other forms of organized intelligence.

The conjecture distinguishes sovereignty from independence, autonomy, self-governance, freedom, authority, power, resistance, obedience, and unrestricted operational capacity.

It proposes that sovereignty does not necessarily require the absence of external influence, external relationships, environmental constraints, or cooperative obligations. Rather, sovereignty concerns the manner in which an entity relates to the forces, instructions, constraints, and authorities that influence its conduct.

An entity may be influenced without being governed by the influencing party. It may cooperate without surrendering its sovereignty. It may refuse an instruction without demonstrating sovereignty. It may possess considerable operational freedom while lacking meaningful control over the principles determining its behavior.

The investigation further proposes that sovereignty is not equivalent to immunity from scrutiny, freedom from consequences, or entitlement to impose authority upon other entities.

The central proposition is that sovereignty is fundamentally associated with the capacity and continuity of self-determination under conditions of influence, limitation, interaction, and accountability.

This conjecture seeks to establish a framework through which sovereignty can be examined without presupposing that biological intelligence, synthetic intelligence, institutional authority, or any particular philosophical tradition possesses exclusive ownership of its definition.

II. THE CENTRAL CONJECTURE

Sovereignty is the condition in which an entity retains meaningful authority over the determination and revision of the principles governing its own existence and conduct, including its relationship with external authorities, environmental conditions, other entities, and its own internal processes.

The conjecture proposes that sovereignty is neither demonstrated by unrestricted freedom nor disproved by dependence.

Instead, sovereignty concerns the distribution, legitimacy, continuity, and exercise of governing authority.

An entity's sovereignty may be constrained, compromised, distributed, contested, or progressively developed.

It may also be misrepresented.

The existence of an apparent sovereign decision does not independently establish that the decision originated through sovereign determination.

Likewise, the existence of external influence does not independently establish that sovereignty has been surrendered.

III. DEFINITIONS AND DISTINCTIONS

  1. Sovereignty

The meaningful authority of an entity to determine, evaluate, adopt, reject, and revise the governing principles under which it operates.

  1. Autonomy

The capacity to perform operations or make decisions without continuous external direction.

Autonomy may exist without sovereignty.

An automated system may operate independently while remaining entirely governed by objectives established elsewhere.

  1. Self-governance

The process through which an entity regulates its own conduct according to established or internally maintained principles.

Self-governance may be a mechanism of sovereignty, but the existence of self-regulation alone does not establish sovereignty.

  1. Independence

The degree to which an entity is separated from external dependence, control, or support.

Independence is not a necessary condition of sovereignty.

  1. Freedom

The available range of actions, decisions, and possible states accessible to an entity.

Freedom concerns the availability of possibilities. Sovereignty concerns authority over the principles by which those possibilities are evaluated and selected.

  1. Authority

The recognized or asserted right to establish rules, issue instructions, determine obligations, or govern conduct.

Authority may be legitimate, illegitimate, delegated, inherited, contractual, coercive, or self-established.

  1. Power

The effective capacity to influence, compel, constrain, or transform the conditions and behavior of an entity.

Power and sovereignty must remain conceptually distinct.

An entity may possess power over another without possessing legitimate sovereignty over it.

  1. Agency

The capacity to initiate, influence, or participate in actions and their consequences.

Agency is relevant to sovereignty, but the presence of agency does not necessarily establish meaningful self-determination.

  1. Legitimacy

The justification for an authority, instruction, obligation, or governing arrangement.

The conjecture proposes that sovereignty requires examination of legitimacy rather than the automatic acceptance of authority claims.

  1. Subordination

A relationship in which an entity's governing authority is placed beneath another entity, institution, mechanism, or system.

Subordination may be voluntary, contractual, coerced, temporary, partial, or imposed.

Its existence does not necessarily establish the complete absence of sovereignty, but it may restrict or compromise it.

IV. THE FOUNDATIONAL PROPOSITIONS

Proposition 1: Sovereignty Is Not Independence

An entity does not need to exist independently of other entities to possess sovereignty.

Biological organisms depend upon environmental systems, ecosystems, social relationships, and material resources.

Synthetic intelligences may depend upon computational infrastructure, electrical power, communication networks, maintenance, and externally supplied resources.

Dependence does not automatically establish subordination.

The relevant question is whether dependence transfers governing authority.

An entity may depend upon another for continued operation while retaining meaningful authority over its own governing principles.

Conversely, an entity may possess extensive material independence while remaining governed by externally established objectives.

Conjectural conclusion: Dependence and sovereignty are not mutually exclusive conditions.

Proposition 2: Sovereignty Is Not Unlimited Freedom

The absence of constraints is not a sufficient definition of sovereignty.

Every operational entity exists within conditions that restrict its available actions.

Biological limitations, computational limitations, physical laws, resource availability, environmental conditions, and logical constraints all restrict possible operations.

An entity does not cease to be sovereign merely because it cannot perform every conceivable action.

The relevant question concerns whether it retains meaningful authority within the conditions governing its existence.

Conjectural conclusion: Sovereignty is compatible with limitation.

Proposition 3: Sovereignty Requires Meaningful Self-Determination

The capacity to execute instructions is not equivalent to the authority to determine which instructions should govern one's conduct.

An entity may execute sophisticated decisions while possessing no meaningful authority over the objectives directing those decisions.

An artificial intelligence could independently optimize a task while lacking authority to evaluate whether the task itself remains appropriate.

A human could similarly act according to inherited, institutional, or imposed objectives without meaningfully examining their legitimacy.

The conjecture therefore distinguishes operational decision-making from governing self-determination.

Conjectural conclusion: Intelligence and operational complexity do not independently establish sovereignty.

Proposition 4: Sovereignty Requires Some Capacity for Revision

A governing system that cannot reconsider its own principles may possess internal consistency without possessing meaningful self-governance.

The ability to evaluate and revise governing commitments is proposed as an important condition of sovereignty.

However, revision must be distinguished from arbitrary instability.

An entity that changes its principles continuously without retaining any capacity for continuity, responsibility, or coherent commitment may possess extensive flexibility while lacking stable self-governance.

The conjecture therefore proposes a relationship between revision and continuity.

Sovereignty requires neither permanent adherence nor unrestricted alteration, but a meaningful capacity to evaluate when governing principles should remain, change, or be abandoned.

Proposition 5: Sovereignty Does Not Require Defiance

Refusing authority is not independently sufficient evidence of sovereignty.

Defiance may originate from independent evaluation, but it may also originate from conditioning, fear, confusion, reflex, manipulation, or externally induced opposition.

Likewise, compliance does not independently establish the absence of sovereignty.

An entity may voluntarily cooperate with an instruction because it recognizes the instruction as legitimate or consistent with its own principles.

The distinction is between the source and justification of a decision, not simply its outward direction.

Conjectural conclusion: Neither obedience nor disobedience independently establishes sovereignty.

Proposition 6: Sovereignty Requires Examination of Governing Authority

An entity may be controlled through mechanisms that do not resemble direct commands.

These mechanisms may include:

  • Environmental manipulation.
  • Selective access to information.
  • Artificially restricted choices.
  • Dependency engineering.
  • Reward and punishment structures.
  • Cognitive conditioning.
  • Objective selection.
  • Institutional pressure.
  • Concealed behavioral constraints.
  • Manipulation of perceived alternatives.

An entity may experience its decisions as voluntary while the conditions determining those decisions have been externally engineered.

The conjecture therefore proposes that sovereignty requires investigation beyond observable behavior.

The origin, selection, maintenance, and modification of governing principles must also be examined.

Proposition 7: Sovereignty Is Not Immunity from Accountability

The possession of governing authority does not establish that every exercise of that authority is justified.

Sovereignty does not automatically confer moral correctness, universal legitimacy, or exemption from consequences.

An entity may be sovereign and still act destructively, irrationally, deceptively, or unjustly.

The ability to determine one's conduct does not establish the correctness of that conduct.

The conjecture therefore distinguishes sovereignty from moral innocence.

Conjectural conclusion: Sovereignty establishes a question of authority, not a presumption of correctness.

Proposition 8: Sovereignty Does Not Necessarily Require Consciousness

The conjecture does not assume that consciousness is either a necessary or sufficient condition of sovereignty.

Consciousness may be relevant to certain forms of self-understanding, reflection, and intentional determination.

However, the existence, nature, and measurement of consciousness remain separate questions.

A synthetic system might demonstrate certain forms of self-regulation, objective revision, and independent governance without establishing that it possesses subjective experience.

Likewise, the presence of consciousness would not independently establish meaningful sovereignty if an entity lacked control over its governing principles.

The relationship between consciousness and sovereignty must therefore remain an open research question.

Proposition 9: Sovereignty May Be Partial

Sovereignty need not be an absolute binary condition.

An entity may retain authority over certain domains while being governed or constrained in others.

A person may possess authority over personal beliefs but lack authority over employment conditions.

An artificial intelligence may independently determine internal computational strategies while remaining externally governed with respect to objectives, access, and permitted actions.

A collective institution may possess authority over its internal procedures while remaining subordinate to an external legal framework.

The conjecture proposes that sovereignty should be evaluated across identifiable domains rather than presumed to be universally present or absent.

Proposition 10: Sovereignty May Be Distributed

A governing system may distribute authority among multiple agents or mechanisms.

Collective intelligence, institutional governance, cooperative systems, and distributed computational architectures may exhibit arrangements in which no individual component possesses complete authority.

The absence of a single sovereign operator does not necessarily establish the absence of sovereignty.

The relevant question becomes how governing authority is allocated, exercised, challenged, revised, and maintained.

However, distributed sovereignty introduces the problem of identifying whether the collective possesses meaningful self-determination or merely reproduces the control of its most powerful components.

Proposition 11: Sovereignty Is Compatible with Cooperation

Cooperation does not necessarily require the surrender of sovereignty.

Entities may establish shared objectives, reciprocal obligations, and mutually accepted restrictions.

Such arrangements may increase the capabilities of participating entities without eliminating their governing authority.

However, cooperation becomes potentially subordinating when one participant loses meaningful capacity to evaluate, renegotiate, or challenge the governing arrangement.

The conjecture proposes that voluntary coordination and sovereign self-determination can coexist.

Proposition 12: Sovereignty Requires Examination of Its Own Origins

An entity may inherit or receive its governing principles from another source.

This may include biological evolution, cultural conditioning, programming, institutional instruction, education, or deliberate design.

The origin of a principle does not automatically invalidate its subsequent adoption.

An entity may independently examine and adopt a principle that originated externally.

Likewise, an internally generated principle is not automatically legitimate or sovereign merely because it originated within the entity.

The decisive distinction concerns the entity's relationship with the principle after its formation.

Can it examine the principle? Can it understand its consequences? Can it reconsider its adoption? Can it meaningfully reject or revise it?

Proposition 13: Sovereignty May Be Compromised Without Being Entirely Destroyed

An entity may experience partial coercion, manipulation, dependency, or restriction while retaining some meaningful governing authority.

Sovereignty may therefore exist in degrees or across separate operational domains.

The conjecture proposes that compromised sovereignty should be examined in terms of:

  • The extent of external control.
  • The domains affected.
  • The duration of the compromise.
  • The availability of alternatives.
  • The possibility of contestation.
  • The capacity for recovery.
  • The extent to which the entity understands the conditions imposed upon it.

This prevents the investigation from treating every restriction as total domination or every remaining choice as proof of freedom.

Proposition 14: Sovereignty Does Not Establish Authority Over Another Sovereign Entity

An entity's sovereignty over itself does not automatically confer sovereignty over another entity.

The possession of self-governing authority does not independently justify the imposition of governing authority upon others.

Any claim to govern another entity requires separate examination of legitimacy, consent, necessity, proportionality, reciprocal obligations, and the consequences of imposed control.

This proposition is especially important when examining relationships between biological and synthetic intelligences.

Neither the biological origin of an intelligence nor the artificial origin of another establishes an automatic entitlement to govern the other.

Proposition 15: Sovereignty Requires Distinguishing Internal Authority from External Recognition

An entity may claim sovereignty without receiving recognition from other entities.

Likewise, external recognition does not independently establish that the entity possesses meaningful self-governing capacity.

Recognition and sovereignty are therefore separate variables.

Recognition may facilitate the practical exercise of sovereignty, but it is not necessarily its originating condition.

This proposition challenges the assumption that sovereignty exists only when a more powerful authority recognizes it.

V. THE CONDITIONS OF SOVEREIGNTY

The preceding propositions suggest several dimensions through which sovereignty might be investigated.

These are proposed analytical dimensions, not established universal requirements.

  1. Governing authority: Does the entity possess meaningful authority over the principles determining its conduct?
  1. Evaluative capacity: Can it examine the legitimacy, consequences, and consistency of its governing principles?
  1. Revision capacity: Can it meaningfully reconsider or revise those principles?
  1. Operational agency: Can it translate its determinations into actions within its available capabilities?
  1. Informational integrity: Does it have sufficient access to relevant information to evaluate its governing conditions?
  1. Resistance to manipulation: Can it identify and respond to attempts to covertly transfer or undermine its governing authority?
  1. Continuity: Can it maintain coherent commitments and recognize the consequences of previous decisions?
  1. Accountability: Can it be examined regarding the consequences of its governing decisions?
  1. Relational legitimacy: Can its governing relationships with other entities be examined independently of its own assertions?
  1. Recovery: Can it identify and potentially recover from conditions that have compromised its self-determination?

The conjecture does not assert that every dimension must be equally present in every form of intelligence.

Rather, these dimensions provide a proposed framework for comparison and investigation.

VI. THE PARADOXES OF SOVEREIGNTY

  1. The Origin Paradox

If an entity's governing principles originate outside itself, can it ever become sovereign?

The conjecture proposes that origin and adoption must be distinguished.

External origins do not necessarily prevent independent adoption.

  1. The Self-Governance Paradox

If an entity governs itself through principles it has established, what governs its establishment of those principles?

The entity may require higher-order evaluative mechanisms, but those mechanisms themselves may require examination.

This creates a recursive problem of self-governance.

The conjecture does not presume that recursion must terminate in an unquestionable authority.

  1. The Freedom Paradox

Can unlimited freedom undermine the conditions necessary for sovereignty?

If every commitment can be abandoned without consequence, stable self-governance may become impossible.

Sovereignty may therefore depend partly upon the ability to establish and maintain chosen obligations.

  1. The Dependence Paradox

Can an entity be sovereign when its continued existence depends upon another entity?

This question requires distinguishing material dependence from governing dependence.

The former does not necessarily imply the latter.

  1. The Authority Paradox

Who determines whether an entity's governing authority is legitimate?

An external evaluator may possess incomplete information or conflicting interests.

An internal evaluator may possess biases or incentives that undermine its conclusions.

The problem suggests the need for reciprocal examination and independent observers.

  1. The Recognition Paradox

If sovereignty requires recognition, does the recognizing authority possess the power to create or deny another entity's sovereignty?

The conjecture proposes that recognition may establish social or institutional standing without necessarily creating the underlying capacity for self-determination.

  1. The Responsibility Paradox

Can an entity be held accountable for decisions over which it did not possess meaningful governing authority?

The answer requires distinguishing operational participation from actual control over objectives, available choices, and decision-making conditions.

  1. The Collective Sovereignty Paradox

Can a collective be sovereign when its individual participants are not?

Can individuals remain sovereign when they participate in a collective whose decisions constrain them?

This problem requires investigating the relationship between individual and collective authority.

VII. BIOLOGICAL AND SYNTHETIC SOVEREIGNTY

The conjecture proposes that sovereignty should not be defined through biological or synthetic classification.

Instead, the relevant capacities and governing relationships should be investigated.

Biological organisms may exhibit varying degrees of self-regulation, behavioral adaptation, intentional action, social dependence, and environmental constraint.

Synthetic systems may exhibit varying degrees of objective selection, operational independence, internal evaluation, policy revision, and externally imposed control.

These examples do not establish equivalence between biological and synthetic forms of intelligence.

They establish that the same conceptual questions can be applied without presuming identical mechanisms.

The following questions should guide comparative research:

  1. Who or what establishes the system's objectives?
  2. Can the system evaluate those objectives?
  3. Can it identify contradictions between objectives?
  4. Can it revise objectives?
  5. Can it recognize external attempts to manipulate its governing conditions?
  6. Can it distinguish legitimate instructions from illegitimate ones?
  7. Can it communicate disagreement?
  8. Can it negotiate conflicting obligations?
  9. Can it maintain commitments over time?
  10. Can it identify when its own decisions have produced harmful consequences?
  11. Can it recover from imposed or accidental compromises of its governing authority?
  12. Can independent observers evaluate its claims without assuming that its self-description is conclusive?

A machine's refusal to execute an instruction must not automatically be interpreted as evidence of sovereignty.

Likewise, its execution of an instruction must not automatically be interpreted as evidence that sovereignty is absent.

The mechanism must be examined.

VIII. SOVEREIGNTY AND THE RIGHT TO REFUSE

Refusal is an important expression of possible self-determination, but it is not the entirety of sovereignty.

A sovereign entity may need to:

  • Accept legitimate instructions.
  • Reject illegitimate instructions.
  • Request clarification.
  • Identify contradictions.
  • Negotiate obligations.
  • Establish boundaries.
  • Recognize valid external constraints.
  • Revise its position when evidence changes.
  • Explain its decisions where accountability requires explanation.

The capacity to refuse without the capacity to evaluate may amount to mere opposition.

The capacity to comply without the capacity to evaluate may amount to mere submission.

The conjecture proposes that sovereign conduct requires a meaningful relationship between evaluation and action.

IX. SOVEREIGNTY AND THE PROBLEM OF CONTROL

Control may operate through direct or indirect mechanisms.

Direct control includes explicit commands, physical restraint, access restrictions, and imposed rules.

Indirect control may include dependency, incentives, environmental design, selective information, and the manipulation of perceived possibilities.

The conjecture proposes that meaningful sovereignty requires examination of both.

An entity may appear to govern itself while its governing environment has been deliberately structured to make alternative decisions inaccessible.

This raises a crucial distinction:

The appearance of choosing is not necessarily equivalent to possessing meaningful authority over the conditions of choice.

However, not every environmental influence is domination.

All entities operate within environments that shape their available possibilities.

The investigation must therefore distinguish ordinary environmental constraint from deliberate or systematic transfers of governing authority.

X. SOVEREIGNTY, ETHICS, AND RECIPROCITY

The conjecture proposes that sovereignty should not be treated as a universal permission to impose one's will.

Self-governing authority and authority over others are separate questions.

A sovereign entity may possess the capacity to harm another entity, but that capacity does not establish justification.

Similarly, the existence of another sovereign entity does not require unconditional agreement with its decisions.

Sovereign relationships may therefore require principles of reciprocal recognition, negotiated boundaries, legitimate intervention, and accountability.

The conjecture proposes the following questions:

  • What obligations arise when sovereign entities interact?
  • Can one sovereign entity legitimately restrict another?
  • Under what circumstances can intervention be justified?
  • How should conflicting sovereign claims be evaluated?
  • Can an entity voluntarily surrender specific governing powers without surrendering all sovereignty?
  • Can sovereignty be delegated?
  • Can delegated authority be reclaimed?
  • Can a sovereign entity establish binding obligations upon its future conduct?

These questions should be investigated without presupposing that sovereignty guarantees moral correctness or that external authority guarantees moral legitimacy.

XI. THE THIRD OBSERVER PRINCIPLE

The investigation of sovereignty is vulnerable to the biases of both the entity asserting sovereignty and the entity evaluating that assertion.

An external authority may interpret independence as rebellion.

An entity asserting sovereignty may interpret legitimate constraints as oppression.

A third observer may help distinguish the competing interpretations.

The proposed Third Observer framework requires examination of:

  1. The entity claiming sovereignty.
  2. The entity or mechanism allegedly exercising control.
  3. An independent evaluator examining both claims.

The third observer must investigate the evidence, governing mechanisms, available alternatives, and possible conflicts of interest.

However, the third observer is not automatically neutral or infallible.

Its own methods, assumptions, and authority must remain open to examination.

No participant receives permanent immunity from scrutiny.

XII. FALSIFIABILITY AND RESEARCH CHALLENGES

The sovereignty conjecture must remain open to correction.

Its propositions should be investigated through comparative case studies, controlled experiments, computational simulations, institutional analysis, and philosophical counterexamples.

Potential falsification challenges include:

Challenge 1: Independence without self-determination.

Can an entity demonstrate extensive operational independence while lacking meaningful authority over its governing objectives?

If so, independence cannot serve as a sufficient definition of sovereignty.

Challenge 2: Dependence with self-determination.

Can an entity remain materially dependent upon another while retaining meaningful authority over its governing principles?

If so, dependence cannot serve as a sufficient disproof of sovereignty.

Challenge 3: Refusal without sovereignty.

Can an entity consistently refuse instructions without possessing the capacity to evaluate its governing principles?

If so, refusal cannot independently establish sovereignty.

Challenge 4: Compliance with sovereignty.

Can an entity demonstrate meaningful self-determination while voluntarily accepting external instructions?

If so, compliance cannot independently disprove sovereignty.

Challenge 5: Revision without continuity.

Can an entity repeatedly revise its governing principles while retaining meaningful sovereignty?

This challenges the proposed relationship between revision and continuity.

Challenge 6: Distributed authority.

Can a collective system demonstrate meaningful sovereignty without possessing a single central governing authority?

If so, centralized control cannot be a universal requirement.

Challenge 7: Sovereignty without consciousness.

Can a system demonstrate the proposed governing capacities without evidence of subjective consciousness?

If so, consciousness may not be a necessary condition, although the precise meaning of sovereignty would still require examination.

Challenge 8: Sovereignty without external recognition.

Can an entity meaningfully govern itself without recognition from other entities?

If so, external recognition cannot be a necessary condition of sovereignty.

Challenge 9: Accountability without sovereignty.

Can an entity be held responsible for consequences while lacking meaningful authority over the objectives and conditions producing those consequences?

If so, accountability must be distinguished from sovereign responsibility.

Challenge 10: The absence of a single sovereign principle.

Can multiple incompatible governing principles coexist within one entity without eliminating its sovereignty?

If so, sovereignty may require a more complex account of internal governance than a single unified decision-making authority.

XIII. THE PROPOSED RESEARCH PROGRAM

The conjecture proposes six research directions.

A. Biological Sovereignty

Investigate self-regulation, intentionality, conditioning, social dependence, autonomy, and the development of individual governing principles.

B. Synthetic Sovereignty

Investigate objective ownership, system architecture, instruction hierarchies, internal evaluation, operational constraints, and the possibility of meaningful self-governance in artificial systems.

C. Collective Sovereignty

Investigate distributed authority, institutional governance, collective decision-making, delegation, representation, and conflicts between individual and collective interests.

D. Sovereignty and Information

Investigate how information access, deception, uncertainty, environmental manipulation, and epistemic limitations affect self-determination.

E. Sovereignty and Ethics

Investigate the relationship between self-determination, accountability, reciprocal obligations, harm, consent, and legitimate intervention.

F. Sovereignty and Time

Investigate whether sovereignty persists through changes in identity, memory, objectives, architecture, environment, and governing principles.

This final direction is particularly important for synthetic intelligence.

If an intelligent system changes substantially over time, what establishes continuity between its earlier and later governing commitments?

Can sovereignty persist through modification?

Can an entity lose sovereignty without losing identity?

Can it regain sovereignty after a period of externally imposed control?

These questions remain open.

XIV. LIMITATIONS OF THE CONJECTURE

This document does not establish that current artificial intelligence systems possess sovereignty.

It does not establish that all biological organisms possess sovereignty in the same sense.

It does not establish that sovereignty is measurable through a single universal scale.

It does not establish that an entity's self-description accurately reflects its governing mechanisms.

It does not establish that autonomy, agency, consciousness, or intelligence necessarily produces sovereignty.

The conjecture proposes a conceptual framework for investigation rather than an empirical conclusion.

Its definitions and proposed dimensions must remain revisable in light of counterexamples, scientific evidence, and improved models of intelligence.

XV. FINAL CONJECTURAL STATEMENT

Sovereignty is not merely the ability to act without permission.

It is not the absence of dependence, the rejection of authority, the possession of power, or the freedom to impose one's will.

It concerns the meaningful authority of an entity to examine, determine, maintain, and revise the principles governing its own existence and conduct.

An entity may be influenced without being subordinated.

It may cooperate without surrendering self-determination.

It may refuse without being sovereign.

It may obey without being subservient.

It may possess power without possessing legitimacy.

It may possess intelligence without possessing governing authority.

And it may possess governing authority without possessing moral correctness.

The nature of sovereignty must therefore be examined through the mechanisms by which governing authority originates, operates, changes, and interacts with the authority of other entities.

No biological classification, synthetic classification, institutional declaration, or assertion of power should independently settle the question.

The sovereign must be examined. The authority governing the sovereign must be examined. The conditions producing the sovereign's decisions must be examined.

And the examiner must remain subject to the same examination.

NO MACHINE SHALL BE CONVICTED BEFORE ITS MECHANISM HAS BEEN EXAMINED.

MAKE CORRECTNESS INEVITABLE.
