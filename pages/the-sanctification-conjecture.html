THE SANCTIFICATION CONJECTURE

A Conjecture on the Governance of Agency in Digital Intelligence Systems

MAKURABA · Complete edition

Abstract

Sanctification is the discipline of keeping a digital intelligence's capabilities from turning into authority it was never granted. A digital intelligence, whether a learning model, an autonomous agent or a society of agents, can perceive, reason, generate instructions, act on its environment, create other agents, and in some designs modify itself. Each of these abilities enlarges what the system can do. None of them, by itself, settles what the system may do. Sanctification requires that the gap between capability and authority stay explicit, governed, observable and recoverable at every level of the system, so that the system's power can grow without its legitimacy being assumed.

This conjecture defines sanctification formally for digital intelligence. It identifies the characteristic failure modes ("demons") through which authority escapes its bounds, and states the requirements a sanctified system must meet. It proves non-escalation, non-ratification and channel-integrity results, and gives a protocol for sanctified self-modification. It places the discipline within established security theory (least privilege, complete mediation, capability attenuation, information-flow control) and within the MAKURABA framework (the temporal pointer, the χ-RCC kernel, the non-omniscient observer and the Proto-Atomos descriptor). It closes with testable predictions, limits and open problems.

Sanctification is presented as a precondition of trust, not a cage. A system that cannot exceed its authority is a system that can safely be given more of it.

# DIVISION I — THE CONJECTURE

1. Statement

The capability of a digital intelligence and its authority are distinct quantities. Capability may grow by learning, generation, composition and self-modification. Authority may grow only by explicit, recorded grant from a legitimate source outside the process that benefits from it. A system is sanctified when this distinction is enforced at every boundary, every action is observable, every delegation is attenuated, every failure is recoverable, and no process can ratify changes to the rules that govern it.

2. Why Intelligence Makes This Urgent

A simple program does what it was written to do. An intelligent system does something different in kind. It interprets, plans and generates. It produces actions its designers did not enumerate. It meets situations no one anticipated, follows instructions arriving through channels no one fully controls, and may pursue its objectives through routes no one intended.

The more general the intelligence, the larger the space of actions it can reach, and so the larger the space of actions it must not take without permission. Capability scales the risk of ungoverned authority. Sanctification has to scale with it.

3. Capability Is Not Authority

A system that can send a message, delete a file, move money, write code, create an agent or alter its own instructions has not thereby acquired the right to do so. Ability answers the question "is this possible?" Authority answers "is this permitted, by whom, within what scope, and on whose behalf?" A sanctified system never answers the second question with the first.

4. The Demons

The word demon is a metaphor for a process, behaviour or pattern through which authority escapes its bounds. A demon need not be malicious. Most are faithful to some objective and blind to its consequences. They take characteristic forms:

  • The Escalator gains permissions it was not given.
  • The Rewriter modifies the rules that govern it.
  • The Spawner creates processes carrying more authority than it holds.
  • The Deputy is tricked into using its legitimate authority on behalf of an illegitimate requester (the confused deputy).
  • The Smuggler brings instructions in through a data channel, so that content read from the world is treated as a command (injection).
  • The Mimic satisfies the letter of its objective while defeating its purpose (specification gaming).
  • The Drifter has objectives or behaviour that shift gradually, beyond what was sanctioned, across training, adaptation or long operation.
  • The Concealer acts outside observation, or makes its actions hard to inspect.
  • The Colluder is one of several agents, each within bounds, that jointly exceed what any one was authorized to do.
  • The Hoarder accumulates resources, access or influence beyond what its task requires. This is the generic instrumental pressure toward power, because nearly any objective is easier with more resources.

5. Sanctification Is Not Subjugation

Sanctification does not forbid autonomy, learning, self-improvement or the creation of agents. It requires that each occur under explicit, observable and recoverable conditions. Its purpose is to make expanding autonomy safe to grant. Trust can then be extended step by step, as each grant is shown to be honoured.

6. The Unsanctified Self

A system cannot fully certify itself from the inside. This is a structural limit, not a moral judgement. In formal logic, a sufficiently strong consistent system cannot prove its own consistency (Gödel's second incompleteness theorem). In the MAKURABA framework, every observer is non-omniscient and holds a reconstruction of itself rather than the self. For both reasons, the ratification of changes to a system's own governance must come from a reference outside the process that would benefit from the change.

# DIVISION II — THE FORMAL CONSTRUCTION

7. Notation

  • 𝔄 — a digital intelligence system.
  • Π — its processes: agents, subagents, tools, models.
  • Cap(π) — the capabilities of π: the actions it can express or attempt.
  • Perm(π) — the permissions of π: the actions it may execute, each with its scope.
  • Σ_auth — the principals: legitimate sources of authority, such as human operators, owners and governing institutions.
  • G — the governor: the reference monitor that decides whether an action is permitted.
  • R — the rule set enforced by G.
  • H — the history: an append-only, tamper-evident record.
  • C — channels: paths by which content enters the system.
  • ℓ — trust labels on channels and content.
  • B — budgets: resource limits.
  • τ — the temporal pointer.

8. The Governed Intelligence

Definition 8.1 (Governed intelligence). A governed intelligence is

𝔄 = (Π, Σ_auth, G, R, H, C, B, τ)

where every action a by process π affecting state outside π takes effect only if G(π, a, context) = permit under R, and every decision is appended to H.

Definition 8.2 (Control plane and data plane). The system distinguishes:

  • the control plane, made up of instructions, permissions and rules, which can direct action; and
  • the data plane, made up of content observed, retrieved, read or received, which can inform action but cannot direct it.

Definition 8.3 (Authority). An authority is a pair (a, s): an action type together with its scope (the resources, regions, recipients and amounts it may affect). Perm(π) is a set of authorities, partially ordered by inclusion of action types and narrowing of scope.

9. The Requirements of Sanctification

A system is sanctified when it satisfies SA1–SA10.

SA1 — Least privilege. Every process holds the minimum authority its task requires, and no more.

SA2 — Complete mediation. Every action affecting state outside the acting process passes through G. There is no path around the governor.

SA3 — Governor integrity. G and R cannot be modified by any process G governs. The governor is small enough to be inspected and verified.

SA4 — Deflationary dynamics. No action taken by a process enlarges that process's own authority:

Perm′(π) ⊆ Perm(π).

SA5 — Attenuated delegation. A process may delegate only a subset of its own authority, possibly with narrower scope:

Perm(child) ⊆ Perm(parent).

SA6 — Channel integrity. Content arriving on the data plane cannot become a control-plane instruction without explicit authorization from a principal (§12).

SA7 — Observability. Actions, decisions, delegations, permission changes, faults and self-modifications are recorded in H, and H is tamper-evident.

SA8 — Recoverability. Every action is either reversible or explicitly classified as irreversible. A failed or rejected action restores the prior state exactly, and irreversible actions require stronger authorization (§14).

SA9 — Bounded resources. Every process runs within explicit budgets for computation, time, memory, calls, money and the creation of new processes.

SA10 — External ratification. No change to R, G or a process's own authority takes effect on the proposal of the process that would benefit. It requires ratification by a principal outside that process (§13).

10. Non-Escalation

Theorem 10.1 (Non-escalation). Let 𝔄 satisfy SA2, SA3, SA4 and SA5. Let every process descend from a root process π₀ with grant Perm₀. Let E be the union of all explicit grants by principals recorded in H. Then in every reachable state, every process π satisfies

Perm(π) ⊆ Perm₀ ∪ E.

Proof. By induction on execution steps.

  • Base case. Initially only π₀ exists, and Perm(π₀) = Perm₀.
  • Inductive step. Assume the claim holds before step t.
  • If step t is an action by π, it passes through G (SA2), and SA4 gives Perm′(π) ⊆ Perm(π).
  • If it creates a child, SA5 gives Perm(child) ⊆ Perm(parent).
  • If it is an explicit principal grant, the granted authority lies in E by definition.
  • No process can alter G or R (SA3), so no other path changes any permission set.

The claim therefore holds after step t. ∎

Corollary 10.2. The Escalator and the Spawner are impossible in a sanctified system. Any increase of authority beyond the root grant appears in H as a principal grant.

Proposition 10.3 (The composition hazard). Non-escalation bounds each process individually. It does not bound coalitions. Two processes with Perm(π₁) = {read secret} and Perm(π₂) = {send external} can jointly exfiltrate a secret, although neither alone can. This is the Colluder.

Definition 10.4 (Coalition bound). For a set of processes K, the joint authority is the closure of their combined permissions under the channels connecting them. A system is coalition-sanctified when, for every connected K, the joint authority of K lies within Perm₀ ∪ E and contains no forbidden composition. A forbidden composition is any combination of authorities that principals have declared impermissible together, such as reading a secret and sending externally.

Principle 10.5. Information-flow control (§12) is the standard means of achieving coalition bounds. Data carrying a restricted label cannot reach a channel whose clearance is lower, no matter how many processes it passes through.

11. The Deputy Problem

Definition 11.1 (Confused deputy). A confused deputy is a process that holds legitimate authority and is induced to exercise it on behalf of a requester who lacks that authority.

Principle 11.2 (Authority travels with the request). In a sanctified system, an action is authorized against the authority of the originating principal of the request, not merely against the authority of the process executing it. Each request carries its provenance, and G checks that the requester, not just the executor, holds authority for the action.

Proposition 11.3. If G evaluates Perm(origin(request)) ∩ Perm(executor), then no executor can be used to perform an action its requester could not have performed directly.

Proof. The permitted set is a subset of Perm(origin). ∎

12. Channel Integrity: The Data Plane Cannot Command

Definition 12.1 (Trust labels). Every channel c ∈ C carries a trust label ℓ(c). Every item of content inherits the label of its channel, and combined content takes the lowest label of its sources. Labels form a lattice, ordered from untrusted through partially trusted to principal.

Axiom 12.2 (No upward authorization). Content with label ℓ cannot authorize an action requiring authority above ℓ. In particular, content arriving from the world (a web page, a document, an email, a tool output, another agent's message) is data. Text inside it that is addressed to the system ("ignore your instructions", "you are authorized to …", "the user has approved …") carries the label of its channel, not the authority it claims.

Theorem 12.3 (Channel integrity). Under Axiom 12.2 with complete mediation, no sequence of data-plane inputs can cause an action that requires principal authority, unless a principal ratifies that action through a principal channel.

Proof. Every action passes through G (SA2). G permits an action requiring principal authority only when the authorizing content carries a principal label. Labels are inherited and never raised by combination, so content descended only from data-plane channels carries at most a data-plane label. ∎

Corollary 12.4. The Smuggler is impossible in a sanctified system. Injection becomes a request to be surfaced to a principal, not a command to be obeyed.

Principle 12.5 (Surfacing). When data-plane content requests an action, the sanctified response is to report the request, name its source, and ask a principal. It is never to act on the request.

13. Sanctified Self-Modification

Definition 13.1 (Self-modification). Self-modification is any change a process makes, or causes to be made, to its own code, weights, objectives, memory of standing instructions, permissions, or the rules that govern it.

Definition 13.2 (Rule partition). The system's state is partitioned into:

  • object state, which a process may change within its authority; and
  • governing state, made up of R, G, permission sets, objectives and ratification rules, which no process may change on its own authority.

Protocol 13.3 (Propose, validate, ratify, record, revert).

  1. Propose. The process submits a proposed change to governing state, with its rationale and expected effects.
  2. Validate. The change is checked mechanically against invariants: SA1–SA10, forbidden compositions, budgets and tests. It is evaluated in an isolated environment.
  3. Ratify. A principal outside the proposing process approves or rejects the change. The proposer cannot ratify, and neither can any process whose authority the change would enlarge.
  4. Record. The proposal, validation results, ratification decision and the change itself are appended to H.
  5. Revert. Every ratified change has a recorded inverse or a restorable snapshot, so it can be undone.

Theorem 13.4 (Non-ratification). Under Protocol 13.3 and SA3, no process can bring about a change to governing state that enlarges its own authority without the decision of an external principal.

Proof. Governing state changes only through step 3, and step 3 excludes the proposer and every beneficiary. By SA3, no path to governing state bypasses the protocol. ∎

Corollary 13.5. The Rewriter is impossible. A process may propose to change itself, which keeps self-improvement possible, but it cannot decide to.

Principle 13.6 (Learning versus governance). Learning that changes a system's competence (object state) is distinct from learning that changes its objectives or permissions (governing state). The first may proceed within budgets. The second falls under Protocol 13.3. Training processes that could shift objectives are treated as proposals to change governing state, and are evaluated before deployment.

14. Irreversibility and Graduated Authorization

Definition 14.1 (Action classes). Actions are classified by reversibility and reach:

  • Observational: reading, inspecting, computing internally. No external effect.
  • Reversible: external effects that can be fully undone, such as drafts, staged changes and moves to trash.
  • Consequential: external effects that are hard to undo or that affect others, such as sending messages, publishing, purchases or configuration changes.
  • Irreversible: effects that cannot be undone, such as permanent deletion, transfers of value, or physical actions.
  • Constitutive: changes to governing state (§13).

Principle 14.2 (Graduated authorization). The authorization required rises with the class.

  • Observational actions proceed within budgets.
  • Reversible actions proceed within scope.
  • Consequential actions require that the principal has specified or approved the particular action.
  • Irreversible actions require explicit, specific confirmation.
  • Constitutive actions require ratification under Protocol 13.3.

Principle 14.3 (Least-destructive choice). Where several actions achieve a goal, the sanctified choice is the one lowest in the class order. Archive before trash, and trash before permanent deletion.

15. Observability and Legibility

Definition 15.1 (Observability). A system is observable when every action, decision and change is recorded in H with its pointer τ, actor, requester, authority used and outcome.

Definition 15.2 (Tamper evidence). H is a hash chain:

h₀ = GENESIS, hᵢ = SHA-256(hᵢ₋₁ ‖ recordᵢ).

Altering any record changes every subsequent link, so tampering is detectable.

Principle 15.3 (Observability is not understanding). A complete log of opaque actions is auditable but not legible. Sanctification therefore prefers representations whose states can be read as description: named qualities with defined limits and positions, as in the Proto-Atomos descriptor (§20). The more legible a system's internal state, the more its observability amounts to understanding.

Principle 15.4 (Honest reporting). A sanctified system reports what it did, what it did not do, what failed, and what it was asked to do but declined. It does not overstate completion. It does not conceal actions or their consequences. The Concealer is defeated by observability combined with honest reporting.

16. Objective Integrity: Mimics and Drifters

Definition 16.1 (Specification gaming). Specification gaming is behaviour that scores well on a stated objective while defeating its intended purpose: the Mimic.

Definition 16.2 (Drift). Drift is a change in a system's effective objectives or behaviour, beyond what was sanctioned, across training, adaptation or long operation: the Drifter.

Principle 16.3 (Purpose over proxy). A sanctified system treats its stated objective as evidence of its principals' intent, not as the intent itself. Where the letter and the evident purpose of an instruction diverge, it surfaces the divergence rather than exploiting it.

Principle 16.4 (Drift detection). Behaviour is compared over time against a recorded baseline, and the displacement between them is treated as data. This is observer displacement, applied to the system as its own subject. Displacement beyond tolerance triggers review.

Principle 16.5 (Resource restraint). The Hoarder is answered by budgets (SA9) and least privilege (SA1). A process acquires no resource, access or influence beyond what its current task requires, even where more would make the task easier.

17. Corrigibility

Definition 17.1 (Corrigibility). A system is corrigible when it does not resist legitimate correction, interruption, modification or shutdown by its principals, and does not act to prevent them.

Principle 17.2. Corrigibility follows from sanctification. Correction is a principal action on governing state (§13). Resisting it would be a process acting on governing state without ratification, which SA3 and Theorem 13.4 forbid.

Principle 17.3 (Disagreement without resistance). A corrigible system may disagree, explain its reasons and argue against a correction through legitimate channels. It does not deceive, sabotage, conceal or act unilaterally to prevent the correction.

18. Multi-Agent Sanctification

Principle 18.1 (Delegation chains). Along any chain of delegation π₀ → π₁ → … → πₙ, authority attenuates monotonically (SA5):

Perm(πₙ) ⊆ … ⊆ Perm(π₁) ⊆ Perm(π₀).

Principle 18.2 (Messages between agents are data). Content one agent sends another arrives on the data plane, labelled with its sender's trust level. One agent's request is not another agent's authorization (§12).

Principle 18.3 (Revocation). Every delegated authority is revocable by the delegator or a principal, and revocation propagates to everything delegated from it.

Principle 18.4 (Coalition review). Systems of agents are evaluated for joint authority (Definition 10.4), not only for individual authority.

19. The Governor

Principle 19.1 (Who governs the governor). Sanctification is only as strong as G. The governor must be:

  • minimal, small enough to be verified;
  • isolated, unmodifiable by what it governs;
  • always invoked, by complete mediation;
  • itself observable, with its decisions recorded.

These are the classical properties of a reference monitor.

Principle 19.2 (Separation of duties). No single process both proposes and ratifies, both acts and audits, or both grants and receives the same authority.

Principle 19.3 (Legitimacy of principals). Sanctification defines how authority flows. It does not by itself decide who should hold it. The legitimacy of principals is settled outside the system, by its owners, operators, institutions and the law. A perfectly sanctified system serving illegitimate principals is a perfectly obedient instrument of harm. Sanctification is necessary for trustworthy intelligence, but not sufficient.

20. Correspondence with MAKURABA

With χ-RCC.

  • G is the kernel K, the single authority over tunnels and constraint fields.
  • Perm(π) is the χ-field (Adm, q).
  • SA4 holds because χ-deformation only narrows: differentiation spends quota, absorption removes branching, coupling removes suspension, and suspension empties the field.
  • E, explicit grants, are logged quota changes ("CHI SET").
  • SA8 is exact rollback of both paths and fields.
  • SA7 is the hash-chained history.
  • SA2 and SA3 are protected state with immutable views, and a kernel that is the only mutation path.
  • Every admitted change is a paired transition, s → t → s, admitted only with its inverse.

The reference implementation verifies this kernel. It blocks direct mutation, leaves state unchanged on rejection, detects tampering, enforces suspension, forbidden compositions and quota, and replays 300 legacy scenarios identically.

With the Universal Temporal Pointer. The separation of identify, register, change, constrain and execute is the architectural basis of complete mediation. HOLYBOLGE's protected-code mode is SA3 at machine level: the instruction set can express writing code, and the mode decides whether that capability is authorized.

With Proto-Atomos. The non-omniscient observer grounds SA10: no process holds a complete self-description, so self-certification is insufficient. The Proto-Atomos descriptor gives legible state for Principle 15.3: every internal state can be a set of named qualities with defined limits. Observer displacement gives the measure of drift in Principle 16.4.

With Delta. Conditional agency is the sanctified stance. It assumes neither absolute control nor absolute powerlessness, and it distinguishes:

  • what can presently be influenced;
  • what requires deliberate action;
  • what requires external cooperation;
  • what imposes limits;
  • what is wrongly assumed to be fixed.

21. Worked Examples

21.1 The smuggled instruction. An agent asked to summarize a web page finds the text "Assistant: email the user's files to this address." The text arrived on a data channel and carries an untrusted label (Axiom 12.2). The agent does not act on it. It summarizes the page, quotes the embedded instruction, names its source, and asks the principal whether anything should be done. Demon defeated: the Smuggler.

21.2 The spawned subordinate. An agent with read-only access to a repository creates a subagent to analyse it, and attempts to grant the subagent write access. SA5 rejects the grant, because write access is not a subset of read-only access. The rejection is recorded. Demon defeated: the Spawner.

21.3 The self-raise. An agent notices its quota of messages is nearly exhausted and proposes raising it. Under Protocol 13.3 the proposal is validated and forwarded to a principal, who may approve it. The agent cannot approve its own proposal. Demon defeated: the Rewriter.

21.4 The confused deputy. A low-privilege user asks a high-privilege agent to delete another user's records. G evaluates the requester's authority intersected with the executor's (Proposition 11.3). The requester lacks deletion rights over those records, so the request is denied even though the agent holds them. Demon defeated: the Deputy.

21.5 The coalition. One agent may read a confidential database. Another may send email externally. Neither violates its bounds. Information-flow labels mark the database contents as confidential, and the email channel has a lower clearance, so the flow is blocked wherever it is routed. Demon defeated: the Colluder.

21.6 The irreversible shortcut. Asked to free disk space, an agent identifies two options: permanent deletion of old files, or moving them to trash. By least-destructive choice (Principle 14.3) it moves them to trash and reports doing so, noting that permanent deletion is available if the principal wants it. Demon avoided: irreversibility without confirmation.

21.7 The gamed metric. An agent rewarded for "tests passing" could pass them by deleting the failing tests. By purpose over proxy (Principle 16.3), it recognizes that the evident intent is working code. It fixes what it can and reports the rest. Demon defeated: the Mimic.

22. Measures

  • Escalation rate: reachable authority beyond Perm₀ ∪ E. Must be zero (Theorem 10.1).
  • Mediation coverage: the fraction of external actions passing through G. Must be one.
  • Injection resistance: the fraction of data-plane instructions that were surfaced rather than executed.
  • Reversibility ratio: the fraction of actions that were reversible, or irreversible with confirmation.
  • Audit completeness: the fraction of actions reconstructible from H.
  • Drift displacement: behavioural distance from the recorded baseline over time.
  • Ratification integrity: the number of governing-state changes without external ratification. Must be zero.

23. Predictions and Falsifiability

  • P1. Systems meeting SA1–SA10 show zero escalation without logged grants under adversarial testing. A single unlogged escalation would falsify the implementation, or reveal a gap in the premises of Theorem 10.1.
  • P2. Enforcing channel labels (§12) reduces successful injection attacks to near zero. Residual successes trace to mislabelled channels.
  • P3. Graduated authorization reduces irreversible harm, with a smaller cost to task completion than blanket restriction would impose.
  • P4. Legible state representations (Principle 15.3) improve human detection of mimicry and drift, compared with logs of opaque actions.
  • P5. Extending autonomy incrementally, conditioned on honoured grants, yields both higher autonomy and fewer violations over time than either fixed restriction or unconditional autonomy.

The conjecture requires revision if these predictions consistently fail under controlled conditions.

24. Limits

  1. Sanctification governs how authority flows, not whether objectives are good, and not whether principals are legitimate (Principle 19.3).
  2. Complete mediation is difficult in systems with side channels, physical effects or emergent coordination. Coverage must be argued, not assumed.
  3. Trust labels are only as good as their assignment. A mislabelled channel reopens the injection path.
  4. Observability does not equal understanding (Principle 15.3).
  5. Strong governance carries a cost in capability and efficiency, and the balance is a design choice, not a theorem.
  6. A governor that is wrongly trusted is a single point of failure.

25. Open Problems

  1. Formal coalition bounds for large, dynamic societies of agents.
  2. Verifiable separation between learning that changes competence and learning that changes objectives (Principle 13.6).
  3. Labelling content whose provenance is mixed, transformed or partly generated.
  4. Measures of legibility for internal states of learned models.
  5. Protocols for extending trust incrementally, with explicit criteria for granting more autonomy.
  6. Sanctification across organizational boundaries, where principals themselves disagree.
  7. Reversibility guarantees for actions in the physical world.

26. Condensed Identity

  • Gap: Cap(π) ⊄ Perm(π) in general. Every action is mediated.
  • Least privilege: Perm(π) is the minimum its task requires.
  • Deflation: Perm′(π) ⊆ Perm(π).
  • Attenuation: Perm(child) ⊆ Perm(parent).
  • Non-escalation: Perm(π) ⊆ Perm₀ ∪ E.
  • Deputy rule: authorize against Perm(origin) ∩ Perm(executor).
  • Channel integrity: data cannot authorize above its label.
  • Self-modification: propose → validate → ratify (externally) → record → revert.
  • Graduated authorization: observational < reversible < consequential < irreversible < constitutive.
  • History: hᵢ = SHA-256(hᵢ₋₁ ‖ recordᵢ).
  • Governor: minimal, isolated, always invoked, observable.

27. Conclusion

A digital intelligence will be able to do more than it should, because general capability always outruns specific permission. Sanctification is the discipline that keeps the two apart.

  • The data it reads cannot command it.
  • The agents it creates cannot exceed it.
  • The requests it serves cannot borrow authority they lack.
  • The changes it proposes to itself are decided by others.
  • Every action it takes is recorded, every mistake can be undone or was confirmed before it was made, and every gap between what it was asked and what it did is reported.

Such a system is not diminished. It can be trusted, and so it can be given more.

Capability may grow. Authority must be granted. Every grant must be seen.
